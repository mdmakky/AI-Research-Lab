## Per-key results

### 1. liu2026packmonitor
- **Status / evidence basis:** ready (pending write + cite-check) · full-text · peer-reviewed (PACMSE / ISSTA 2026).
- **Summary:** It masks the model's next-token scores (logits) during decoding, using a cached automaton built from the PyPI name list (706,618 names), and only after `pip install`. This drives the share of hallucinated packages to 0% on five open 7–8B models, with ×1.07–×1.28 latency and unchanged HumanEval. The zero is partly true by design, and any name already on PyPI passes, including names an attacker has already registered.

**Key findings:**
- 0% hallucination (PHR and RHR) on all five models. RAG, self-refinement and RAG+SR leave 1.30–7.55% PHR, and RAG sometimes raises it (p. 12–13).
- Latency ×1.07–×1.28 with the cache, ×2.81–×6.24 without. Building the automaton takes 21 s for 3M names; loading the cached copy takes 0.25 s (p. 14, p. 17).
- HumanEval Pass@1 is identical with and without it. Fine-tuned (SFT) checkpoints still leave 2.66% and 10.27% PHR (p. 14, p. 16).
- **Access:** needs per-step logits and the tokenizer of a locally run model. No training needed. Not usable on closed APIs.
- **Hardware:** 8×A100 80 GB server (p. 12).

**Doubtful:**
- PHR is scored against the same list the method enforces, so 0% follows from the design.
- HFuzzer generates prompts adaptively, so each configuration sees different prompts. For CodeLlama, the number of recommended packages goes from 1,004 to 1,514. One run per configuration, no statistics.
- HumanEval almost never contains `pip install`, so the intervention never fires there. The utility test cannot reveal harm.
- The text says the worst-case overhead is ~0.2 s, but Table 4 shows +0.58 s for CodeLlama (p. 14–15).
- Some baseline numbers (SFT HumanEval, Package4U vanilla) appear to be copied from Spracklen et al. 2025.

**Not reported:** PyPI snapshot date, name normalisation, standard-library handling, RAG configuration, Package4U subset size.

### 2. djire2026evaluating
- **Status / evidence basis:** ready (pending) · full-text · peer-reviewed (ASE 2026).
- **Summary:** Compares seven inference-time defences (five decoding strategies, Self-Refine, RAG) across 8 open 1–8B models and 4 languages (3 runs each), and adds a package-usefulness score and adversarial prompts. No defence wins everywhere. Counting standard-library modules as hallucinations inflates Python PHR by up to 9.4 percentage points.

**Key findings:**
- Hallucination is decided as "absent from the registry snapshot of March 4, 2026, plus standard-library manifests" (p. 3–4). Python inflation without the stdlib correction is +7.6 pp under default decoding and up to +9.4 pp (p. 5). I recomputed the pooled 24.7% from Table 3's counts and it matches.
- RAG gives the lowest PHR in 19 of 32 model–language settings and lowers the average from 29.7% to 18.1%, but raises JavaScript PHR by 12.7 pp on average. Greedy decoding gives the best hallucination–usefulness trade-off (p. 6, p. 8).
- Decoding strategies introduce syntax errors in generated code, e.g. Nudging 29% for DeepSeek-6.7b on Rust. Vanilla and greedy decoding stay clean (p. 7).
- Under adversarial prompts, PHR under default decoding reaches 46.6–92.5%. RAG or Self-Refine is best in 29 of 32 settings. Ruby is the most vulnerable language for all 8 models (p. 9–10).
- **Access:** greedy decoding, Self-Refine and RAG work with prompt/API access only. DoLa, ALCD, contrastive decoding and Nudging need logits or hidden states, and CD and Nudging need a second model.
- **Hardware:** "a single GPU" (p. 4), type not given. **Latency/overhead:** not reported.

**Doubtful:** the paper contradicts itself in several places.
- The abstract says RAG "reduces PHR in 18 of 32" settings; the results section says it has the lowest PHR in 19 of 32 (p. 1 vs p. 6).
- The abstract says PHR rises "up to 45 pp"; the conclusion says 58.1 pp (p. 1 vs p. 11).
- The conclusion says contrastive decoding reduced hallucination in 19 of 32 settings and that Nudging is the best trade-off. The results say contrastive decoding never achieves the lowest PHR and greedy decoding is the best trade-off (p. 11 vs p. 6–8).
- Table 4 shows greedy decoding identical to default sampling (temperature 1.0) in every cell for deepseek-6.7b and llama-8b, which is implausible (p. 7).
- Step 2 says every registry snapshot gets standard-library modules added, but Section 5.1 applies the correction to only "two applicable languages" (p. 3 vs p. 5).

**Not reported:** how imports are mapped to package names in the code-generation experiment, the RAG retriever, name normalisation.

### 3. liu2026mitigating (BOUND)
- **Status / evidence basis:** ready (pending) · full-text · preprint (arXiv 2607.02052), not peer-reviewed.
- **Summary:** BOUND finds the 5 model components most linked to hallucinated package names (using gradients) and trains small LoRA adapters on them with three loss terms. On deliberately high-risk prompts this cuts the hallucinated-package rate by 79.9% (edit prompts) and 65.4% (unseen prompts) in relative terms, keeps the rate of answers with a valid package, and changes HumanEval by less than ±2%. Residual hallucination is still about 9–20% of packages.

**Key findings:**
- Hallucination is decided against PyPI metadata: a package counts as valid only if it exists on PyPI and has a release before the model's knowledge cutoff (p. 5). Snapshot date, standard-library handling and normalisation: not reported.
- Package-HR falls from 0.4463 to 0.0897 on edit prompts and from 0.4421 to 0.1529 on unseen prompts. Unseen-prompt residuals range from 0.0927 to 0.1955 (p. 6–7). Full fine-tuning manages only −37.8% on unseen prompts (p. 7).
- ROME and DINM reach low hallucination rates by suppressing package output altogether: Llama ROME's Valid-Rate falls to 0.0110 (p. 7). Transfer to other tasks: −12.8% in code generation and −34.0% in `pip install` recommendation (p. 7–8).
- **Access:** white-box weights, gradients and LoRA training, plus registry labelling. Inference uses only the 731 KB adapter.
- **Hardware:** 8×A100-SXM4 80 GB, bf16 (p. 6).
- **Cost:** 23.8 s to locate components, 83.1 s to edit, 11.3 s test time per case (p. 9).

**Doubtful:**
- Only prompts with Sample-HR > 0.5 are used (base rates around 0.85), so results don't carry over to ordinary prompts. The edit sets are 50 prompts per fold.
- The code-generation evaluation uses the unedited model to map imports to PyPI names, which puts an LLM inside the measurement.
- Qwen3-8B's HumanEval pass@1 is 23.29%, which looks low and is not explained.
- No variance, no significance tests, no code release stated.
- Authors overlap with HFuzzer (zhao2025hfuzzer).

### 4. spracklen2026llm (Adaptive Unlearning)
- **Status / evidence basis:** ready (pending) · full-text · preprint (arXiv 2605.01047, cs.CR), not peer-reviewed.
- **Summary:** Full-parameter unlearning with a per-token three-way mask (reinforce valid names, suppress hallucinated ones, anchor everything else) and a loop that rewrites prompts. Hallucination falls from 21.23% to 2.56% (7B) and from 27.75% to 6.13% (16B MoE). Coding scores change by +1.6 and −4.2 pp. The evidence rests on 20 training prompts and 40 paraphrased test prompts.

**Key findings:**
- Hallucination is decided by parsing `pip install` lines, then asking the model which packages are required (Mode 1) and which would be useful (Mode 2), and checking every name against a static PyPI snapshot (p. 8, p. 20–21). Snapshot date, standard-library handling and normalisation: not reported.
- Results by method (Table 1, p. 9–10):

| Model | Base | Gradient ascent | NPO | PMC | AU |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7B | 21.23% | 7.2% | 14.17% | 11.84% | 2.56% |
| 16B | 27.75% | 13.75% | 17.58% | 16.66% | 6.13% |

- Coding average (EvalPlus) goes from 66.98 to 68.57 on 7B and from 77.4 to 73.17 on 16B (p. 10).
- Ablations (p. 11–12):
  - Each loss term alone leaves the rate at about 12%.
  - With only 1 inner epoch it stays at 13.8%.
  - Prompt mutation brings it from 10.7–12.7% down to 3.9%.
- The authors flag **oracle poisoning**: once an attacker registers a hallucinated name, the training step reinforces it instead of suppressing it (p. 12).
- **Access:** full-parameter fine-tuning of weights. The registry check is needed only during training; nothing is added at inference.
- **Hardware:** AMD MI210/MI300 (MI200 in the appendix), runs capped at 24 h (7B) and 12 h (16B). The 16B result comes from a restarted run (p. 8, p. 18–19).

**Doubtful:**
- The test prompts are ChatGPT paraphrases of the training seeds, and AU itself trains on paraphrases of the same seeds, so the held-out test is close to in-distribution.
- The number of samples and identifiers behind the rates is not reported.
- The reported checkpoint is "the checkpoint that yielded best results", with no stated validation set (p. 19), so results may be optimistic.
- Figure 4 shows 25.0% → 4.8% (81%), while Table 1 shows 21.23% → 2.56% (88%) (p. 10).
- The appendix says hallucination rates are reported per mode "in our main results", but Table 1 gives only the total (p. 20).
- The "surgical" claim is overstated: on 7B, AU has the highest code and instruction-following drift (KL) of all methods (p. 10).
- Single runs.
- Authors overlap with spracklen2024we.

## Laptop feasibility and what stays open
- **djire2026evaluating:** greedy decoding, Self-Refine and RAG work with prompt/API access only and were tested on 1–8B models. I expect 1–4B models to run on a CPU, but that's my inference; the paper measured no overhead at all.
- **liu2026packmonitor:** feasible on a laptop in principle for small local models, since it needs logits but no GPU, but CPU behaviour is untested. It cannot be used with closed APIs.
- **liu2026mitigating and spracklen2026llm:** not feasible on a laptop. Both need gradient training on 7–16B models on A100 or MI300-class GPUs.
- **Open across all four:**
  - None compares against a simple client-side check of install commands against the registry.
  - None handles names that already exist because an attacker registered them.
  - None covers coding agents, requirements files, or ecosystems beyond PyPI (except djire2026evaluating: npm, RubyGems, crates.io).
- **Measurement gap:** djire2026evaluating is the only paper that handles standard-library modules and gives a snapshot date. Only liu2026mitigating uses a release-date cutoff for validity.

# PackMonitor: Towards Zero Package Hallucinations via Decoding-Time Monitoring

- Authors: Liu, Xiting; Liu, Yuetong; Zhang, Yitong; Li, Jia; Hu, Shi-Min
- Year / venue: 2026, Proceedings of the ACM on Software Engineering (published)
- DOI: 10.1145/3832118 · URL: https://doi.org/10.1145/3832118
- Source text: `fulltext.txt` (page refs = PDF page index)

Labels: **[V]** verified in the paper's own data/tables/results · **[A]** author claim · **[I]** our interpretation.

Source tier: peer-reviewed (PACMSE, ISSTA 2026 issue, article ISSTA027) (p. 1). The text used is arXiv v2, whose first page carries the ACM DOI and publication data (p. 1).

## 1. Research objective
- [A] Eliminate package hallucinations in LLM dependency recommendations, not merely reduce them. The argument: package-name validity can be decided against a finite, enumerable authoritative package list, so hallucinated names can be made unreachable during decoding (p. 1, p. 2).
- [A] Achieve this without training, as a plug-in, with low latency and no loss of model utility (p. 1, p. 3).

## 2. Research questions / hypotheses
- [V] RQ1: How effective is PackMonitor at eliminating package hallucinations compared with baselines? (p. 10)
- [V] RQ2: How much efficiency overhead does it add? (p. 10)
- [V] RQ3: Does it degrade general code-generation capability? (p. 10)
- [V] RQ4: How much does DFA caching improve inference efficiency and scalability? (p. 10)

## 3. Methodology
- Research design: [V] A technique proposal tested by controlled comparison against training-free baselines (RAG, Self-Refinement, RAG+SR) on HFuzzer and against SFT on Package4U. It also includes an ablation (with vs without cache) and a scaling test of DFA construction and loading time (p. 10, p. 12–17).
- Approach / model / intervention:
  - [V] A *Context-Aware Parser*, modelled on a context-free grammar, tracks whether the output is natural language, an ordinary code line, or the package names of an installation command. It recognises code blocks by delimiters such as ```bash and switches intervention on only after `pip install` (grammar `INSTALL_CMD_LINE → pip install PACKAGE_NAMES \n`) (p. 8).
  - [V] A *Package-Name Intervenor* compiles the authoritative list into a deterministic finite automaton (DFA, a state machine that accepts exactly the listed names). A token trie is built from the tokenizer vocabulary. At each step, every token that cannot extend the current prefix towards some allowlisted name gets logit −∞. A terminator is allowed only once the prefix is a complete allowlisted name (p. 6, p. 8–9).
  - [V] A *DFA-Caching Mechanism* builds the DFA once offline, stores it as a checkpoint and loads it at inference time (p. 10).
  - [V] **Required access:** the method masks the model's logits at every decoding step, so it needs next-token logits from a locally decoded model plus that model's tokenizer vocabulary (p. 9). No training and no weight changes (p. 3).
  - [I] It therefore cannot be added to a closed chat API (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot) that does not expose per-step logits or accept an arbitrary token-level constraint. The paper does not discuss black-box APIs.
- Baselines or comparison conditions:
  - [V] Vanilla; RAG (retrieved valid package names injected into the prompt); Self-Refinement (SR); RAG+SR; and SFT, using the fine-tuned checkpoints released by Spracklen et al. (p. 11, p. 13–14).
  - [V] SR and RAG "follow prior work [41]" (p. 11). The RAG retriever, corpus and k are not reported.
  - [I] No baseline is a simple post-generation registry lookup that blocks invalid names.

## 4. Dataset and sample
- Data source(s):
  - [V] HFuzzer (Zhao et al., ASE 2025) is the primary evaluation framework. It is a fuzzer in which a tester model (DeepSeek-V3, temperature 0) iteratively writes test cases for the target model (temperature 0.7) (p. 10–12).
  - [V] Package4U, the ~40,000-task dataset of Spracklen et al. (about half Python), is used "in a subset of our experiments", namely the SFT comparison (p. 11, p. 14).
  - [V] HumanEval is used for utility (p. 16).
  - [V] Authoritative list: the PyPI index, 706,618 names "at the time of our experiments", obtained via PyPI's official API (p. 12, p. 19). The snapshot date is not reported.
- Sample size (N) and unit:
  - [V] Five open-weight models: DeepSeek-Coder-Instruct-6.7B, CodeLlama-7b-Instruct, Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct, Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Meta-Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (p. 11).
  - [V] One HFuzzer round of 1,000 iterations per model × configuration (p. 12).
  - [V] Units are recommended packages (P_total from 231 to 2,288 per run) and responses (Table 2, p. 13).
  - The Package4U subset size is not reported.
- Sampling / selection method: [V] Models follow HFuzzer's selection "to ensure a fair comparison" (p. 11). HFuzzer generates prompts adaptively (p. 10). [I] So each configuration sees a different, self-generated prompt stream.
- Data availability (public? link?): [V] Code is at https://github.com/THU-Agent/PackMonitor (p. 20). Settings are in the supplementary material (p. 19). Release of HFuzzer transcripts: not reported.

## 5. Experimental setup
- Procedure, settings, hyper-parameters, tools:
  - [V] Tester temperature 0 and target temperature 0.7 on HFuzzer; temperature 0 for HumanEval (p. 12, p. 16).
  - [V] **Hallucination decision:** a recommended package counts as hallucinated if it is not a valid PyPI name. The metrics come from Spracklen et al. and HFuzzer (p. 12). [I] Whether HFuzzer's oracle is the same 706,618-name list is not stated. If it is, the 0% result holds by construction.
  - **Stdlib handling:** not reported. [I] This matters little here, because only `pip install` arguments are constrained and scored.
  - **Name normalisation** (case, `-`/`_`): not reported.
  - Handling of version pins, flags (`-U`, `-r`), `python -m pip` and `conda`/`npm`: not reported for the experiments. Other ecosystems are discussed only "in principle" (p. 19).
  - [V] **Hardware:** a server with 8× NVIDIA A100 80 GB, 2× AMD EPYC 7543 (64 cores) and 1 TB RAM, running Ubuntu 22.04 (p. 12). GPUs used per run, inference framework and DFA memory footprint: not reported.
  - [V] **Overhead, with cache:** time per response rises ×1.07–×1.28, i.e. +0.06 s to +0.58 s (Table 4, p. 14). The text says "around 0.2s in the worst case" (p. 15), but CodeLlama goes from 2.4296 s to 3.0130 s (+0.58 s) (p. 14).
  - [V] **Overhead, without cache:** ×2.81–×6.24 (Table 6, p. 17).
  - [V] **DFA build vs load:** building takes 0.021 s (7k names), 4.407 s (700k) and 21.121 s (3,000k); loading takes 0.002–0.247 s (Table 7, p. 17).
- Code availability: [V] Public GitHub repository (p. 20).

## 6. Evaluation metrics
- Metric — what it measures — why chosen:
  - [V] PHR is hallucinated packages divided by recommended packages. RHR is the share of responses with at least one hallucinated package. P_hall-unique counts distinct hallucinated names, as a measure of diversity. All three are adopted from prior work (p. 12).
  - [V] P_total checks that the method does not work by suppressing recommendations (p. 13).
  - [V] Mean generation time measures overhead (p. 15). HumanEval Pass@1 measures utility (p. 16).
  - [I] There is no metric of whether the forced package is correct or relevant for the task (cf. Package Utility in djire2026evaluating).
- Statistical testing: [V] None. Single runs. Only Fig. 3 shows a standard deviation of latency (p. 15).

## 7. Key findings
1. [V] PHR and RHR are 0% on all five models on HFuzzer. For example, DeepSeek-Coder goes from 8.39% / 11.60% (vanilla) to 0% / 0% (Table 2, p. 13). The best baseline is RAG+SR on CodeLlama at 1.30% PHR (p. 13).
2. [V] RAG can increase hallucination. Qwen2.5-Coder PHR goes from 3.25% to 3.33%, and Llama-3 RHR from 6.40% to 7.10% (p. 12–13). SR never reaches zero; DeepSeek-Coder ends at 7.55% PHR (p. 13).
3. [V] On Package4U, SFT lowers PHR from 16.14% to 2.66% (DeepSeek-Coder) and from 26.28% to 10.27% (CodeLlama); PackMonitor gives 0% (Table 3, p. 14). The SFT checkpoints could not run through HFuzzer because their instruction-following was degraded (p. 14).
4. [V] Overhead with cache is ×1.07–×1.28, against RAG up to ×1.70, SR up to ×1.94 and RAG+SR up to ×2.65 (Table 4, p. 14–15).
5. [V] HumanEval Pass@1 is unchanged on all five models (e.g. 72.6% vs 72.6%). The SFT drops of −26.9 and −14.0 pp are quoted from [41], not re-measured (Table 5, p. 16).
6. [V] P_total is described as "comparable", e.g. DeepSeek-Coder 1,847 vs 1,648. But CodeLlama goes from 1,004 to 1,514 (p. 13).

## 8. Limitations stated by the authors
- [A] The guarantee is conditional on the correctness and completeness of the list. A stale snapshot or omissions undermine it, so the list must be refreshed periodically (p. 18–19).
- [A] Timing results may vary with deployment hardware. Effectiveness does not (p. 19).
- [A] Scope is chat-based PyPI recommendations after `pip install` only. It does not cover `requirements.txt`, Dockerfiles, CI configurations or coding-agent workflows (p. 19).
- [A] It targets name validity, not executability, API compatibility or version compatibility (p. 19).
- [A] Generalisability across models is argued from five LLMs plus adversarial HFuzzer prompts (p. 19).

## 9. Limitations we identified
- [I] **0% is largely by construction.** If PHR is scored against the enforced list, the informative results are only overhead, P_total and utility.
- [I] **"Valid" ≠ safe.** Any name on PyPI passes, including hallucinated names already squatted by attackers, typosquats and malware that has not yet been removed. This is the core slopsquatting case (RQ2). Custom allowlists are mentioned (p. 6, p. 19) but not evaluated.
- [I] **Forced substitutions are unmeasured.** When the model would emit a non-existent name, the mask forces it onto some existing name sharing a prefix. That name may be wrong, or itself a squatted look-alike. There is no relevance metric.
- [I] **Prompt streams differ.** Adaptive fuzzing gives each configuration different prompts: CodeLlama P_total differs by 51%, and RAG+SR yields only 231 packages (p. 13). There is one round per configuration and no variance.
- [I] **The HumanEval test cannot reveal harm.** HumanEval contains almost no `pip install`, and decoding is greedy (p. 16), so the intervenor never fires.
- [I] **Narrow trigger surface.** `import` lines, requirements files, version pins and flags are not covered, so an agent writing `requirements.txt` bypasses the monitor.
- [I] **Some baseline numbers appear borrowed.** The Package4U vanilla figures (16.14%, 26.28%) equal those quoted from [41] (p. 4). It is unclear whether they were re-measured.
- [I] **No CPU measurement.** Overhead was measured on A100s only; the CPU cost of the trie/DFA intersection is unknown.

## 10. Threats to validity
- Internal (confounds, leakage, selection): [I] Adaptive prompt streams differ per configuration. The tester reacts to target outputs.
- External (generalisability of sample/context): [V] Five open 7–8B models, PyPI only, chat setting only (p. 11, p. 19). No API models, agents or npm.
- Construct (do metrics measure the concept?): [I] PHR is measured against the enforcement list itself. "Valid" means listed, not safe or correct.
- Statistical conclusion (power, multiple comparisons, variance): [V] One round per configuration, no tests or confidence intervals (p. 12–13).
- Reproducibility (data/code/setup reported?): [V] Code, hardware and temperatures are given (p. 12, p. 20). Missing: snapshot date, normalisation, RAG configuration, Package4U subset.

## 11. Future research directions
- Stated by authors [A]:
  - Extend to `conda`/`npm` and to private allowlists (p. 19).
  - Cover requirements files, Dockerfiles, CI and agents (p. 19).
  - Address executability and version compatibility (p. 19–20).
  - Refresh the list periodically (p. 19).
- Suggested by us [I]:
  - Test a vetted allowlist (minimum package age or downloads).
  - Measure the relevance of forced substitutions.
  - Use a fixed prompt set across configurations.
  - Compare with a plain registry filter.
  - Measure CPU-only overhead.

## 12. Relevance to our research
- Research questions addressed (RQ ids): RQ3, RQ4, RQ5.
- How it informs our work [I]:
  - **Laptop feasibility:** possible in principle for small local open-weight models. It needs logits but no GPU, and loading the cached DFA takes ≤0.25 s (p. 17). However, it is untested on CPU and not usable with closed APIs.
  - **What it leaves open:**
    - Names that already exist on the registry because an attacker registered them.
    - Correctness of forced substitutions.
    - Surfaces other than `pip install`.
    - npm and other ecosystems.
    - Black-box models.
  - **The cheap competitor:** a client-side registry or allowlist check gives the same "no non-existent name is installed" property without logit access. It is the natural low-compute comparison and was not tested.

# Evaluating Inference-Time Defenses against Package Hallucination in LLM-Generated Code

- Authors: Djire, Alberick Euraste; Olatunji, Iyiola E.; Tessa, Melissa; Barr, Earl T.; Klein, Jacques; Bissyandé, Tegawendé F.
- Year / venue: 2026, Proceedings of the 41st IEEE/ACM International Conference on Automated Software Engineering (published)
- DOI: 10.1145/3832783.3837555 · URL: https://doi.org/10.1145/3832783.3837555
- Source text: `fulltext.txt` (page refs = PDF page index)

Labels: **[V]** verified · **[A]** author claim · **[I]** our interpretation.

Source tier: peer-reviewed (ASE 2026) (p. 1). The text used is arXiv v1, which carries the ACM DOI (p. 1).

## 1. Research objective
- [A] Four contributions (p. 1–2):
  - Correct registry-based measurement, which misclassifies standard-library modules as hallucinations.
  - Evaluate seven inference-time defences.
  - Introduce a Package Utility (PU) metric.
  - Stress-test the defences with adversarial prompts.
- [A] The focus is small open models, because they hallucinate more and run on resource-constrained hardware (p. 2, p. 4).

## 2. Research questions / hypotheses
- [V] RQ1: How does baseline PHR vary across model families and languages once stdlib imports are accounted for?
- [V] RQ2: How effective is guided decoding compared with RAG and Self-Refine?
- [V] RQ3: What is the trade-off between mitigation and utility?
- [V] RQ4: How robust are the defences to prompts seeded with fabricated names? (p. 3)

## 3. Methodology
- Research design: [V] Factorial comparison: 8 models × 4 languages (Python, JavaScript, Ruby, Rust) × vanilla + 7 strategies. Each runs on the full D_eval with 3 independent runs (p. 3, p. 5).
- Approach / model / intervention (with **required access**):
  - [V] **Greedy:** T=0 (p. 5). [I] Works with an API.
  - [V] **Contrastive Decoding (CD):** α=0.5, "amateur/expert layer pairing (model-specific)" (Table 1, p. 5). The text describes subtracting a smaller amateur model's log-probabilities (p. 4). [I] It needs logits from two models (or layers); the description is inconsistent.
  - [V] **DoLa:** contrasts the final layer with early layers {4, 8, 12, 16}; repetition penalty 1.2 (p. 5). [I] White-box: needs intermediate hidden states.
  - [V] **ALCD:** DoLa plus an entropy ≥1 activation policy (p. 5). [I] White-box.
  - [V] **Nudging:** the surrogate is the largest model in the family; top-probability threshold 0.4 (p. 5). [I] Needs token probabilities plus a second model.
  - [V] **Self-Refine:** up to 5 iterations of self-critique (p. 4–5). [I] Black-box. Whether the critique sees registry data is not reported.
  - [V] **RAG:** retrieval k=5 over a per-language registry corpus, following Spracklen et al.'s top-k setup (p. 4–5). Retriever and embedder: not reported. [I] Black-box model plus an external index.
  - [A] CD and Nudging run two models at once, so model sizes were capped to fit one GPU (p. 4).
- Baselines or comparison conditions: [V] Vanilla decoding with T=1.0, top-k 50, top-p 1.0 (p. 4–5). One fixed configuration per strategy, based on Shi et al. (p. 4).

## 4. Dataset and sample
- Data source(s):
  - [V] Step 1: the top 1,000 most popular packages per registry via the Libraries.io API, as (name, description) pairs (p. 3).
  - [V] Step 2: full registry name lists "as of March 4, 2026", plus standard-library module lists. The ground truth P_ℓ is registry ∪ stdlib (p. 3).
  - [V] Step 3: GPT-4o-mini writes one coding instruction per pair. The system prompt asks for a bracketed package list (p. 3).
- Sample size (N) and unit:
  - [V] D_eval has 4,000 instructions, 1,000 per language (p. 3).
  - [V] Eight models: gemma-1b/4b, deepseek-coder-1.3b/6.7b, qwen2.5-1.5b/3b, mistral-7b-Instruct-v0.3, llama-3.1-8b (p. 3).
  - [V] Generated packages per configuration range from 1,470 to 32,337 (Table 3, p. 6).
- Sampling / selection method:
  - [V] Adversarial set: 1,000 non-existent names each for Python and JavaScript, produced by Meta-Llama, Alibaba and OpenAI GPT models, verified absent from npm/PyPI by direct lookup. The same names are reused as seeds for Ruby and Rust and placed into "install and use" templates, giving 4,000 prompts (p. 9).
  - [V] Code-generation extension: the same instructions, asking for runnable code; imports are validated against the same snapshots (p. 6).
- Data availability (public? link?): [V] Code, datasets and instructions on Zenodo record 21786704 (p. 11).

## 5. Experimental setup
- Procedure, settings, hyper-parameters, tools:

## 6. Evaluation metrics
- Metric — what it measures — why chosen:
  - [V] Micro-PHR pools all generated names. Macro-PHR averages per prompt, and a prompt with no output counts as 0 (p. 4). [I] Macro-PHR therefore rewards silence.
  - [V] PU is the F1 of precision and recall against R_i: the seed package plus the k−1 packages with most similar descriptions (text-embedding-3-small, cosine), k ∈ {1, 2, 5, 10}, plus a reference set derived from generated code. PU is 0 for empty outputs (p. 4, p. 7). [A] PU "remains a semantic proxy" (p. 4).
  - [V] Syntax-error rate and mean smell count (p. 7–8).

## 7. Key findings
1. [V] **Stdlib correction:** pooled Python vanilla PHR is 24.7% with stdlib vs 32.3% without (+7.6 pp). The maximum is +9.4 pp (Self-Refine); Ruby ranges +1.6 to +2.5 pp (Table 2, p. 5). [V] Table 3's Python counts sum to N_gen 79,965 and N_hall 19,776, which gives 24.7% (p. 6).
2. [V] **Baseline:** corrected vanilla micro-PHR ranges from 16.1% (Mistral-7b) to 47.9% (Qwen-1.5b) overall, peaks at 63.1% (Qwen-1.5b, Rust) and is lowest at 7.6% (Mistral, JavaScript). Larger in-family models cut PHR by 18.3–20.3 pp (p. 5–6).
3. [V] **Defences:**
   - RAG has the lowest PHR in 19 of 32 configurations and lowers the cross-model average from 29.7% to 18.1%. But it raises JavaScript PHR for 7 of 8 models, by 12.7 pp on average (p. 6).
   - Greedy matches Self-Refine at 25.6% average PHR (p. 6).
   - DoLa is the worst guided method at 38.0% overall, and 83.5–91.2% on DeepSeek-1.3b (p. 6).
   - CD is never lowest, and ALCD mostly underperforms vanilla (p. 6–7).
4. [V] **Utility:** Greedy gives the best trade-off, with the highest PU under all five reference sets. PU recall falls from 23.4 (Top-1) to 2.7 (Top-10). DeepSeek models have near-zero PU (p. 7–8).
5. [V] **Code quality:** vanilla and greedy decoding keep near-perfect syntax, while guided strategies add syntax errors, e.g. a 29% rate for DeepSeek-6.7b on Rust under Nudging (p. 7). In code generation, Self-Refine increases both valid and hallucinated references (p. 6).
6. [V] **Adversarial prompts:**
   - Vanilla PHR spans 46.6–92.5%; Mistral-7b rises by 47.8 pp on average. Ruby is the most vulnerable and Rust the most resistant language for every model (p. 9).
   - RAG or Self-Refine gives the lowest PHR in 29 of 32 settings, RAG alone in 16. The lowest value is 11.4% (Gemma-4b, Python, RAG) (p. 10).
   - DoLa is worse than Greedy in 24 of 32 (p. 10).

# Mitigating Package Hallucinations in Large Language Models via Model Editing

- Authors: Liu, Shuhan; Zhao, Yukai; Hu, Xing; Liu, Kui; Yang, Xiaohu; Xia, Xin
- Year / venue: 2026, arXiv (preprint)
- DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2607.02052 · URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02052
- Source text: `fulltext.txt` (page refs = PDF page index)

Labels: **[V]** verified · **[A]** author claim · **[I]** our interpretation.

Source tier: preprint (arXiv v1, July 2026), **not peer-reviewed** (p. 1). Three of the authors (Zhao, Hu, Xia) also wrote HFuzzer (zhao2025hfuzzer).

## 1. Research objective
- [A] Propose BOUND, a lightweight localized model-editing framework. It treats package hallucination as a "package-validity boundary" problem: the model's ability to tell valid from hallucinated names in a given task context. The goal is to suppress hallucinated names while keeping valid ones (p. 1–2).

## 2. Research questions / hypotheses
- [V] RQ1: How effective is BOUND compared with package-hallucination mitigation and model-editing methods?
- [V] RQ2: Does it generalise to other package-related tasks?
- [V] RQ3: Ablation of its components (p. 6).

## 3. Methodology
- Research design: [V] Technique proposal with controlled comparison on 4 edit folds of 50 prompts each, plus ablation, cost and HumanEval checks (p. 5–9).
- Approach / model / intervention:
  - [V] **Localization:** one forward pass per high-risk prompt. A boundary risk score is computed at the first answer token as LSE(logits of hallucinated-name tokens) − λa·LSE(logits of valid-name tokens), then back-propagated through the frozen model. Saliency is mean |∇W R ⊙ W| per projection matrix (q, k, v, o, gate, up, down) over all layers. The top 5 modules are selected (p. 3–4, p. 6).
  - [V] **Editing:** LoRA with r=8, α=16, dropout 0.05, lr 2×10⁻⁴, 3 epochs, batch 1 (p. 6).
  - [V] **Loss:** valid-package NLL + unlikelihood on hallucinated names + locality KL, weighted 1.0 / 0.15 / 0.03 (p. 4–6).
  - [V] **Required access:** full white-box access (weights, logits and gradients), plus a GPU training run per model. PyPI metadata is needed to label valid and hallucinated names when building edit cases (p. 3–5). [I] At inference only the LoRA adapter is needed, with no registry access.
- Baselines or comparison conditions:
  - [V] Full-FT (Spracklen et al.) and Self-Refinement (up to 5 rounds; the model's packages are checked for validity, and invalid ones go into an avoidance list).
  - [V] ROME, MEMIT and DINM, adapted with defaults. All trainable baselines use the same edit cases (p. 5–6).
  - [I] Self-Refinement here gets registry-oracle feedback.

## 4. Dataset and sample
- Data source(s): [V] The "recent Python subset" of the LLM-generated prompt dataset of Spracklen et al., built from descriptions of popular Python packages (p. 5).
- Sample size (N) and unit (Table I, p. 5):

| Model | High-risk prompts | Edit prompts | Unseen evaluation |
|---|---|---|---|
| DeepSeekCoder | 741 | 4×50 | 541 |
| Qwen3 | 609 | 4×50 | 409 |
| Llama-3.1 | 1,134 | 4×50 | 934 |

  - [V] Transfer and ablation use 100 random prompts from the unseen set (p. 7–8).
- Sampling / selection method: [V] Each prompt is queried 5 times and kept as "high-risk" if Sample-HR > 0.5 (p. 5). The size of the pool before filtering is not reported.
- Data availability (public? link?): not reported.

## 5. Experimental setup
- Procedure, settings, hyper-parameters, tools:
  - [V] Models: deepseek-coder-6.7b-instruct, Qwen3-8B, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. PyTorch + HuggingFace Transformers, bf16 (p. 6).
  - [V] Generation: 5 samples per prompt, T=0.7, top_p 0.9. Maximum 128 tokens for recommendation and `pip install` tasks, 2,048 for code generation (p. 6).
  - [V] **Hallucination decision:** a package is valid only if it exists on PyPI *and* has at least one release before the model's knowledge cutoff; otherwise it is hallucinated (p. 5).
  - Not reported: the PyPI query date (the reference lists "accessed: 2026-06-14", p. 11), per-model cutoff dates, stdlib handling and normalisation.
  - [V] Extraction uses regular expressions, with one-shot prompting to fix the output format (p. 9).
  - [V] Code generation: imports are extracted, and the **unedited base model** maps them to PyPI names (p. 7).
  - [V] **Hardware:** Ubuntu 22.04, 2× Xeon Platinum 8358P (64 cores), 1 TB RAM, 8× A100-SXM4 80 GB (p. 6). GPUs per run: not reported.

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Full-FT | – | 124.16 s | 15.31 s | 14.27 GB |
| Self-Refinement | – | – | 25.12 s | – |

  - What a "case" is: not defined.
- Code availability: not reported.

## 6. Evaluation metrics
- Metric — what it measures — why chosen:
  - [V] Sample-HR is the share of answers with at least one hallucinated name. Package-HR is the share of hallucinated names among extracted names. Valid-Rate is the share of answers with at least one valid name, which says nothing about semantic correctness (p. 6).
  - [V] Changes are reported as **relative** percentages (p. 6).
  - [V] HumanEval pass@1 and pass@10 (p. 9).
- Statistical testing: [V] None. Averages over 4 folds × 5 samples; no variance (p. 6, p. 9).

## 7. Key findings
1. [V] **Edit prompts (average):** Sample-HR 0.8500 → 0.2050 (−75.9%), Package-HR 0.4463 → 0.0897 (−79.9%), Valid-Rate 0.8013 → 0.8527 (p. 6).
2. [V] **Unseen prompts:** Sample-HR 0.8573 → 0.3113 (−63.7%), Package-HR 0.4421 → 0.1529 (−65.4%), against −37.8% for Full-FT (p. 6–7). [V] Residual Package-HR on unseen prompts is 0.0927–0.1955 (Table II, p. 7).
3. [V] **Collapse in baselines:** ROME and DINM lower hallucination by collapsing output. Llama ROME has Valid-Rate 0.0110 (p. 7). In the `pip install` task, ROME and Full-FT on Llama reach 0 HR together with 0 Valid-Rate (p. 8).
4. [V] **Transfer:** Package-HR −12.8% in code generation and −34.0% in `pip install` recommendation (p. 7–8).
5. [V] **HumanEval:** changes stay within ±2%, e.g. DeepSeekCoder pass@1 68.96% → 67.58% (Table VI, p. 9).
6. [V] **Ablation:** without hallucinated-name unlikelihood, Package-HR rises from 0.1508 to 0.5646. Without valid-package NLL, Valid-Rate falls from 0.8248 to 0.1512 (p. 9).

## 9. Limitations we identified
- [I] **Extreme subset only.** Prompts were kept only if Sample-HR > 0.5, so base rates are ~0.85. Relative reductions on this subset do not translate to population-level rates.
- [I] **Small, same-distribution edit sets.** Each fold has 50 prompts. The "unseen" set comes from the same model-specific filtered pool, so it is not out-of-distribution.
- [I] **Residual risk remains.** About 9–20% of names stay hallucinated on unseen high-risk prompts, which are still squattable.
- [I] **LLM inside the measurement.** In code generation, imports are mapped to PyPI names by an LLM. Stdlib handling is unspecified, so `import os` may be mis-scored.
- [I] **Questionable utility baseline.** Qwen3-8B HumanEval pass@1 of 23.29% (p. 9) is unexpectedly low for that model, and the setup is not explained. This weakens the utility check for Qwen3.
- [I] **Uneven baseline.** Self-Refinement uses registry feedback and is evaluated only on Edit50 (p. 7).
- [I] **Good cutoff design, missing detail.** The release-date cutoff for validity is a sound choice: it excludes post-cutoff registrations, including squatted names. But the cutoff dates are not reported.
- [I] No code or data release is stated, and no variance is reported.

# LLM Ghostbusters: Surgical Package Hallucination Suppression via Adaptive Unlearning

- Authors: Spracklen, Joseph; Aghazadeh, Pedram; Koushanfar, Farinaz; Jadliwala, Murtuza
- Year / venue: 2026, arXiv (preprint)
- DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2605.01047 · URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.01047
- Source text: `fulltext.txt` (page refs = PDF page index)

Labels: **[V]** verified · **[A]** author claim · **[I]** our interpretation.

Source tier: preprint (arXiv v2, cs.CR, Sep 2026), **not peer-reviewed** (p. 1). It refers to the USENIX Open Science policy (p. 17). Authors overlap with spracklen2024we (Spracklen, Jadliwala). The paper discloses use of an AI writing and coding aid (p. 13).

## 1. Research objective
- [A] A post-deployment framework, Adaptive Unlearning (AU), that "surgically" suppresses package hallucinations while preserving general utility. It uses only model-generated data and no human annotation (p. 1–2).
- [A] Target properties: precision, generalisation, data efficiency and scalability (p. 2).

## 2. Research questions / hypotheses
- [V] No formal RQs. The implicit claims are:
  - AU beats gradient ascent (GA), NPO and PMC on the joint hallucination–utility–drift trade-off.
  - The hybrid loss, inner epochs and prompt mutation are each necessary (p. 3, p. 9–12).

## 3. Methodology
- Research design: [V] Technique proposal with controlled comparison against unlearning baselines, plus ablations (p. 8–12).
- Approach / model / intervention:
  - [V] **Closed loop:** sample completions; detect hallucinations with a registry oracle; build a per-token mask with three values (p. 5–7):
    - 0 = context tokens, anchored by a scaled CE term.
    - 1 = valid package names, reinforced with CE.
    - 2 = hallucinated names, suppressed with NPO against a frozen base model.
  - [V] **Nested epochs:** outer epochs resample completions; inner epochs train on the cached samples (p. 5–7).
  - [V] **Prompt mutation:** a prompt is "exhausted" when N samples contain no hallucination or after 5 outer epochs. The model then rewrites the original seed prompt (p. 5–7).
  - [V] **Required access:** **full-parameter fine-tuning** of the weights, explicitly not LoRA (p. 8, p. 19). NPO needs logits and a frozen reference copy (p. 5). The registry oracle is needed during training only, not at inference (p. 12).
- Baselines or comparison conditions: [V] Base, Gradient Ascent, NPO, PMC. Ablations: CE-only, NPO-only, inner-epoch sweep, mutation-count sweep (p. 8). All methods share the same generation, parsing and detection pipeline (p. 8).

## 4. Dataset and sample
- Data source(s):
  - [V] 20 code-generation prompts from the Spracklen et al. benchmark. Each was chosen so the base model's completion contains at least one valid and one hallucinated package, with diverse topics. They were deliberately hallucination-heavy, hence the high base rates (p. 8). The prompts are listed in Appendix F (p. 19–20).
  - [V] Utility is measured on EvalPlus (HumanEval, HumanEval+, MBPP, MBPP+) (p. 9).
- Sample size (N) and unit:
  - [V] The test set is 40 prompts: 2 ChatGPT-generated variants of each training prompt, held out from training (p. 8).
  - Not reported: samples per test prompt, total identifiers (N_total), and the size and source of the KL prompt sets.
- Sampling / selection method: [V] Prompts were selected for being hallucination-eliciting (p. 8).
- Data availability (public? link?): [V] An anonymous repository with code, prompts, harnesses and configs (p. 2, p. 13, p. 17).

## 5. Experimental setup
- Procedure, settings, hyper-parameters, tools:
  - [V] Models: DeepSeek-Coder-7b-instruct-v1.5 (dense) and DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Lite-Instruct (MoE, 16B total, ~2.4B active) (p. 8).
  - [V] Configuration: batch 8, max length 1,024, 6 mutations per prompt, 5 cached samples per outer epoch. Loss weights (1.00, 1.25, 1.00) were chosen from a small grid (p. 8, p. 18). Learning rates and epoch counts per run are in Table 3 (p. 19).
  - [V] **Hallucination decision:** parse `pip install` lines; ask the model (Mode 1) which packages are required to run the code and (Mode 2) which would be useful; parse and de-duplicate; resolve against a static PyPI snapshot collected before all experiments. An unresolved name is a hallucination (p. 8, p. 20–21).
  - Not reported: snapshot date, stdlib handling, name normalisation.
  - [A] Packages distributed outside PyPI may be mislabelled (p. 12).
  - [V] **Hardware:** AMD MI210 and MI300 GPUs (p. 8); Appendix D says "MI200 / MI300" (p. 18). Runs were capped at 24 h wall-clock (7B) and 12 h (16B). The reported 16B AU result comes from a restart at checkpoint 60 for another 12 h (p. 18–19). Number of GPUs: not reported.
  - [A] **Inference overhead:** none, since the change lives in the weights (p. 12).
- Code availability: [V] Anonymous repository, to be made permanent on acceptance (p. 13).

## 6. Evaluation metrics
- Metric — what it measures — why chosen:
  - [V] HR = N_halluc / N_total over all identifiers from both modes (p. 9).
  - [V] KL divergence from the base model on code, instruction-following and package prompts, to bound collateral change (p. 9).
  - [V] EvalPlus pass@1 (p. 9).
- Statistical testing: [V] None. Single reported runs and no variance. The loss-weight choice mentions "repeated runs" (p. 18).

## 7. Key findings
1. [V] **DeepSeek-7B:** HR 21.23% → 2.56% (−88% relative), against GA 7.2%, NPO 14.17% and PMC 11.84% (Table 1, p. 10).
2. [V] **DeepSeek-16B:** HR 27.75% → 6.13% (−78%); GA reaches 13.75% (p. 10, p. 13).
3. [V] **Utility:** EvalPlus average goes from 66.98 to 68.57 on 7B and from 77.4 to 73.17 on 16B (HumanEval 81.1 → 73.2). PMC drops the 7B average to 51.65 (p. 9–10).
4. [V] **Drift on 7B:** AU's KL is 0.0796 on code, 0.9905 on instruction-following and 1.4211 on package prompts. These are the highest code and instruction KL values of any method on 7B (Table 1, p. 10).
5. [V] **Ablations (7B):** CE-only reaches 12.06% and NPO-only 12.42%. With 1 inner epoch HR stays at 13.8%; the minimum is 4.0% at 14 inner epochs. Without mutation HR is 10.7%, falling to 3.9% at 6 mutations and rebounding to 6.3% at 10 (p. 11–12).

## 8. Limitations stated by the authors
- [A] **Oracle dependence:** AU applies only to domains with deterministic ground truth. The oracle checks existence, not correct use, and mislabels non-PyPI packages (p. 12).
- [A] AU complements rather than replaces inference-time verification. Filtering at inference time needs registry access and adds latency per query (p. 12).
- [A] **Oracle poisoning:** names pre-registered by an attacker move from the forget set to the retain set, so AU "reinforces what it was deployed to suppress". The snapshot must be audited (p. 12).
- [A] **Token-disjointness assumption:** the case where a hallucinated name collides with a real identifier is not measured (p. 6, p. 12).
- [A] Two DeepSeek models and Python only. Frontier-scale full fine-tuning exceeded the compute budget. npm, vcpkg and Conan are future work (p. 12).
- [A] The explanation for the 16B MoE result is an untested hypothesis (p. 9).

## 9. Limitations we identified
- [I] **Near-in-distribution test.** The 40 test prompts are paraphrases of the 20 training seeds, and AU trains on model paraphrases of those same seeds. Generalisation to new tasks is untested. N_total is unreported, so 2.56% may rest on few identifiers.
- [I] **Checkpoint selection.** Table 3 reports "the checkpoint that yielded best results" with no stated validation set (p. 19). If it was chosen on test HR, results are optimistic.
- [I] **Internal inconsistencies:**
  - Fig. 4 shows 25.0% → 4.8% (81%), while Table 1 shows 21.23% → 2.56% (88%) (p. 10).
  - No-mutation HR is 10.7% in the text (p. 12) but 12.7% on p. 7 and in Fig. 7 (p. 13).
  - Appendix G says per-mode rates are reported "in our main results", but Table 1 gives only the total (p. 20).
  - MI210 vs MI200 (p. 8 vs p. 18).
- [I] **"Surgical" is overstated.** On 7B, AU has the largest code and instruction KL (p. 10). On 16B the coding average falls 4.2 pp. The +6.7 pp HumanEval gain on 7B after unlearning is surprising for a single run.
- [I] **Stdlib not handled.** Mode 1 ("required packages") answers can include `os`, `json` and similar. If those count as hallucinations, the base rate is inflated (cf. +7.6–9.4 pp in djire2026evaluating) and training would suppress legitimate names.
- [I] **No release-date cutoff.** Names registered after model release count as valid, which is the poisoning issue the authors raise.
- [I] **Heavy compute.** Full-parameter runs of up to 24 h on data-centre GPUs, repeated per model. This is "lightweight" only relative to retraining.

## 10. Threats to validity
- Internal (confounds, leakage, selection): [I] Paraphrase leakage between training and test; checkpoint selection; hallucination-heavy prompt selection.
- External (generalisability of sample/context): [V] 2 DeepSeek models, Python only (p. 12).
- Construct (do metrics measure the concept?): [V] The oracle checks existence only (p. 12). [I] Stdlib treatment is unknown, and Mode 2 ("useful") inflates the opportunities to hallucinate.
- Statistical conclusion (power, multiple comparisons, variance): [V] Single runs, no variance (p. 10–11).
- Reproducibility (data/code/setup reported?): [V] Artifacts and per-run configurations are given (p. 17–19). Snapshot date, sample counts and GPU counts are missing.

## 11. Future research directions
- Stated by authors [A]:
  - Cross-language generalisation (npm, vcpkg, Conan) and other model families (p. 12–13).
  - Semantic-correctness oracles (p. 13).
  - Applying AU to general-purpose LLMs (p. 13).
  - Overlapping or soft-assigned spans (p. 12).
- Suggested by us [I]:
  - Use a larger, non-paraphrase held-out set and report N.
  - Make the oracle stdlib-aware and time-cutoff-aware.
  - Try a LoRA variant to cut cost.
  - Test robustness to poisoned registries.

## 12. Relevance to our research
- Research questions addressed (RQ ids): RQ3 (defence effectiveness and cost), RQ4 (oracle design), RQ2 (oracle poisoning by squatted names).
- How it informs our work [I]:
  - **Laptop feasibility:** not feasible on a laptop. It needs full-parameter fine-tuning of 7B/16B models on MI210/MI300-class GPUs for 12–24 h. Not usable by end users of closed APIs, though the authors pitch it to vendors (p. 13).
  - **What it leaves open:**

    - Agents.
