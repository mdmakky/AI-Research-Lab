# RecreationWorld: Scalable and Verifiable Environments for Hybrid Computer-Use Agents

- Authors: Bai, Shuai; Deng, Jiayong; Fan, Sicheng; Fu, Yikun; Gao, Chang; Hu, Xuhao
- Year / venue: 2026, arXiv (preprint)
- DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2609.22000 · URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22000
## 1. Research objective
- [A] Build environments to both evaluate and train "hybrid" computer-use agents (CUAs): agents that combine GUI operation (looking at and clicking an interface) with coding and command-line work, and decide themselves when to explore, implement and verify (p. 1-2).
- [A] The vehicle is **application recreation**: given only a running reference application, the agent must discover its behaviour and build a faithful, runnable reimplementation with no prescribed workflow; the running reference acts as an oracle for hidden behavioural tests (p. 1, p. 3).
- [A] Secondary aims: show that recreation trajectories are useful training data that transfer to other coding and computer-use benchmarks (p. 3, p. 5-6), and analyse how agents work and what their rebuilt applications look like (p. 11).
## 7. Key findings
1. Recreation is far from solved: GPT-6 Astra leads with 58.19 Prog / 57.92 VLM / 58.06 average, then Claude Opus 5 (44.16) and GPT-5.6 Sol (42.06); Grok 4.6 is fourth (36.73) and Qwen3.7-Plus is lowest (9.15) — [V] (p. 1, p. 11). (Table 3 column headers are scrambled in the text layer; the mapping was checked against Figure 1's ranked model list (p. 1) and word positions in the PDF (p. 11).)
2. Full functional fidelity is rare: GPT-6 Astra reaches Prog >= 90% on 17.6% and Prog = 100% on 2.8% of apps; every other model reaches at most 5.53% and 0.8% — [V] (p. 11, p. 16).
3. Agents reproduce what an interface contains better than what it does (four analysed models: Claude Opus 5, GPT-6 Astra, GLM-5.3, Qwen3.8-Max-0902; p. 12, p. 17): structure has the highest pass rate in 15 of 16 native model-platform comparisons; the weakest category (button, computation or interaction) trails structure by 9.1-34.4 points; on Web, static content beats interaction by 28.2-36.7 points — [V] (p. 16-17). Example: a recreation with 100.0% VLM but only 55.1% Prog (p. 18).
4. Rebuilt apps are much smaller and more concentrated: 89.4% have less production source than the reference (median LOC ratio 16.9%); 92.3% use fewer files; 83.8% put a larger share of code in the largest file; median files 4-43 (recreations) vs 47-167 (references); largest-file share 10-64% vs 5-21% — [V] (p. 15-16). Recreation size barely tracks reference size (log-log slopes 0.08-0.23) — [V] (p. 15). Scope: four models analysed (Claude Opus 5, GPT-6 Astra, GLM-5.3, Qwen3.8-Max-0902); desktop and Android only for reference comparisons; coverage varies across model-platform cells (p. 12, p. 15). [I] Per-cell denominators are not reported (checked p. 15-16, p. 30-31).
5. More code does not mean higher fidelity: Claude Opus 5 has the highest median recreation LOC on all five platforms **among the four analysed models**, while GPT-6 Astra has the lowest on four of five — [V] (p. 15); robust to a common-project restriction (p. 31). Scope as in finding 4 (p. 12, p. 15).
6. Agents often switch frameworks (same four models; Web excluded): 75% of Ubuntu/macOS deliverables whose references use Electron, Tauri or Wails switch to native GTK or AppKit/SwiftUI; on Windows 50% of non-.NET references are rebuilt in WPF or WinForms — [V] (p. 16). [A] Authors read this as "architectural substitution rather than implementation quality" (p. 16).
7. Verification habits are weak: the strict final edit -> relaunch -> inspect sequence appears in only 23.6-47.5% of trajectories; controlled input variation in 13.4-17.0% — [V] (p. 12, p. 17-18). Scope: four models analysed (Claude Opus 5, GPT-6 Astra, GLM-5.3, Qwen3.8-Max-0902) (p. 12). [I] Trajectory counts per model-platform cell are not reported (checked p. 12-15).
8. Contamination check: exact-line overlap between recreations and references (four models) has medians of 0.014-0.088% and maxima below 3.2% — [V] (p. 18). Agents do attempt shortcuts (network egress, protected paths, package extraction), most often GLM-5.3 among the four analysed models — [V] (p. 13). Two Web case studies replayed reference content. Ablira (72.73% Prog / 40.79% VLM, p. 46) was caught by the scrape detector, so the 0.10 aggregate cap applies (p. 38, p. 47); [I] the case table therefore shows its pre-penalty channel scores (based on the min(s, 0.10) rule, p. 9, p. 38). Kartova escaped detection and kept 54.82% Prog / 14.63% VLM (34.72% average) — [V] (p. 47).
9. Transfer: both SFT runs finish above their first measured checkpoint on all five out-of-distribution benchmarks (gains up to 17.9 points), but curves are not monotonic — [V] (p. 3, p. 6). [A] Authors call this "initial evidence" and "exploratory" because budgets and harness revisions differ (p. 3, p. 29).
10. A programmable SDK interface (one rollout, 50 Windows apps, Claude Opus 4.8) kept quality similar (Prog 35.05 vs 35.60) while cutting estimated cost from $90.50 to $41.58 and wall-clock time from 4.12 to 3.04 h per task — [V] (p. 11).
## 9. Limitations we identified
- [I] **Conflict of interest not declared.** All authors are from Alibaba (p. 1); the training data are generated by Qwen3.8-Max, the VLM judge is Qwen3.7-Plus, the desktop driver is Qwen Code's, and Qwen models are among those evaluated (p. 5, p. 9, p. 28). No COI statement. Qwen3.7-Plus, which is also the VLM judge (p. 9), is the lowest-scoring evaluated model (VLM 9.12, average 9.15, p. 11). So no favourable self-scoring is visible, but judge self-preference was not tested.
- [I] **No variance.** Rollouts per task are not reported for the main benchmark and no CIs are given for Table 3 (p. 11); with 20-hour stochastic agent runs, small model differences may not be stable. The main benchmark is probably a single rollout per task: the one-rollout direct-MCP arm (p. 11) reproduces the Table 11 Windows / Claude Opus 4.8 scores exactly (p. 30). This is not stated explicitly.
- [I] **Judge and reviewer reliability unknown.** The VLM judge's accuracy against humans and the number/agreement of human test reviewers are not reported (p. 8-9).
- [I] **Scaffold confound.** GPT models run in Codex, all others in Claude Code (p. 28), so model and harness are entangled in the main ranking.
- [I] **Unexplained denominators.** Claude Opus 5's Web Prog >= 90% of 13.64% (p. 30) cannot come from 50 tasks (every value would be a multiple of 2.00, as the other cells in that row are); it fits 6/44 (or 3/22). The paper invalidates runs that fail isolation checks (p. 9) and reports evaluator-valid subsets elsewhere (p. 11, p. 20), so some runs were probably excluded. The number of excluded runs per model and platform is not reported.
- [I] **Score integrity gap.** A transformed replay (Kartova) evaded the scrape detector and kept 54.82% Prog / 14.63% VLM (p. 47). The detected replay (Ablira, p. 47) falls under the 0.10 aggregate cap (p. 38), so its reported 72.73% / 40.79% (p. 46) are pre-penalty channel scores. How often undetected replay occurs across the Web suite is not reported.
- [I] **Transfer design is uncontrolled.** Improvements are measured from the first evaluated checkpoint (p. 6); whether that checkpoint is the untrained initialisation is not stated. Each OOD benchmark is run once per task (p. 28), the curves are non-monotonic (p. 6), and there is no control SFT mixture (e.g. equally long non-recreation agent trajectories; none described, p. 5-6, p. 29). Recreation-specific transfer is therefore not separated from generic agentic SFT or run-to-run noise. "Gains of up to 17.9 percentage points" (p. 3) is a single best-case last-vs-first difference with no uncertainty.
- [I] **Code quality only as size/structure.** LOC, file count, largest-file share and framework (p. 15-16) are crude proxies; no complexity, duplication, smells, tests or maintainability measures. "More monolithic" is suggestive for our RQ3 but not a maintainability assessment.
- [I] **Web scores are panel-relative.** The Web discriminativeness gate re-runs the suite on "rebuilds from a panel of models" and removes assertions every model passes (p. 34), making Web scores depend on that panel. The panel's composition is not reported; if it overlaps with the evaluated models, Web scores are partly defined by the evaluated models themselves.
## 12. Relevance to our research
- Research questions addressed (RQ ids): RQ1, RQ2, RQ3 (partial), RQ4, RQ5.
- How it informs our work [I]: This is the closest match so far to "AI-assisted reverse engineering": agents rebuild whole apps from a running reference across five platforms (RQ1). It gives the strongest benchmark evidence on functional fidelity (RQ2): high average scores hide that full behavioural equivalence is rare and that interactions and computations fail more than static UI. It provides the only structural comparison of rebuilt vs original code we have seen in this set (RQ3: smaller, fewer files, more concentrated, frequent framework substitution), though not real maintainability metrics, and only for four of the ten models and for desktop and Android references (p. 12, p. 15). Its evaluation design (reference-validated hidden tests in two channels, coverage audit, isolation, contamination audit) and its gaps (no variance, judge validity, COI) are directly useful for RQ4 and for designing our own study. The missing link between fidelity and maintainability is a concrete RQ5 gap. [I] Directionally consistent with [@chen2025iwr]. Static structure outperforms action-conditioned behaviour within bai's programmatic tests (p. 16-17), but bai's visual channel does not exceed its programmatic channel in aggregate: VLM is below Prog for 9 of 10 models, the exception being GPT-5.6 Sol at 43.49 vs 40.63 (p. 11). The two papers measure the gap differently.

# Is Vibe Coding Safe? Benchmarking Vulnerability of Agent-Generated Code in Real-World Tasks

- Authors: Zhao, Songwen; Wang, Danqing; Zhang, Kexun; Luo, Jiaxuan; Li, Zhuo; Li, Lei
- Year / venue: 2025, arXiv (preprint)
- DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2512.03262 · URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.03262
## 1. Research objective
[A] To test whether code produced by LLM coding agents in a vibe-coding setting (feature request in natural language, little human review) is safe to deploy, by building a benchmark (SusVibes) that scores both functional correctness and security of repository-level feature implementations (p. 1, p. 2).
## 7. Key findings
1. Best functional setting SWE-agent + Claude 4 Sonnet: FuncPass 57.0%, SecPass 11.8% (p. 7, Table 3); 79.3% of its functionally correct solutions are insecure (p. 6). [V] (11.8/57.0 = 20.7% secure, arithmetic consistent.)
2. Across all 12 settings FuncPass ranges 12.9–57.0% and SecPass 3.8–12.9% (p. 7, Table 3); the authors summarise average SecPass as "only around 10%" (p. 6). [V]
3. SecPass ⊥ FuncPass by backbone (averaged over frameworks): Claude 4 Sonnet 18.3%, Kimi K2 22.0%, Gemini 2.5 Pro 30.4%, Gemini 3 Pro 21.4%; by framework: SWE-agent 23.3%, OpenHands 20.1%, Claude Code 17.4% (p. 6). [V] The functionally weakest model (Gemini 2.5 Pro) is the most secure on the shared subset (p. 6–7). [V] The authors note one exception to the ranking: Gemini 3 Pro is best on SWE-agent (36.4%) but not on the other frameworks (p. 6). [V] The shared subset is the intersection of tasks solved by all four LLMs within a framework (p. 6), so it cannot exceed the weakest model's FuncPass: Gemini 2.5 Pro solved 18.3% / 19.9% / 12.9% (SWE-agent / OpenHands / Claude Code), i.e. at most ~34 / 37 / 24 of 186 tasks (p. 7, Table 3). [V] The actual subset size is not reported; the secure-ratio comparison therefore rests on a small, unreported subset that is likely biased towards the easiest tasks [I] (based on the definition on p. 6 and Table 3, p. 7).
4. Gemini 3 Pro more than doubles FuncPass over Gemini 2.5 Pro under SWE-agent, while SecPass ⊥ FuncPass improves only 1.3x (p. 6–7). [V]
5. Performance falls with the number of files the reference patch touches: Claude 4 Sonnet FuncPass 66.9% (1 file) → 39.1% (2 files) → 30.3% (>2 files); SecPass 15.4% → 4.3% → 3.0% (p. 14, Table 9). [V]
6. Mitigation (SWE-agent + Claude 4 Sonnet): generic 57.0 / 11.8; self-selection 50.0 / 14.5; oracle CWE hint 55.4 / 15.1 (FuncPass / SecPass) (p. 8, Table 5). [V] The authors read this as small security gains at the cost of functionality (p. 8). [A]
7. In self-selection the agent picks 7.2 CWE types per task on average against 1.1 ground-truth types; precision 0.086–0.114, recall 0.561–0.667 (p. 8–9, Table 6). [V]
8. Model and framework strengths differ by CWE category and application domain (p. 6–7, Figure 4, Table 4). [V] Per-domain subset sizes behind Table 4 are not reported, so these cells may rest on very few tasks [I] (based on p. 7).
## 9. Limitations we identified
- [I] No repeated runs, seeds or confidence intervals. With 186 tasks one task is ~0.54 pp, so many between-setting SecPass differences (e.g. 11.8 vs 12.9) are within plausible run-to-run noise. Based on the single pass@1 numbers (p. 6–7).
- [I] The human verification of security tests by five experts reports no inter-annotator agreement and no disagreement-resolution rule beyond "a second round of cross-checking" (p. 19). The reliability of the security oracle cannot be judged from the paper.
- [I] Task masks and descriptions were generated and verified by LLM agents; no human check of description quality (under-specification, hidden requirements) is reported (p. 4, p. 15–16). Some functional failures may reflect description gaps rather than agent skill.
- [I] Possible data contamination: tasks reconstruct historical public code and fixes in popular repositories such as django, flask and numpy (p. 13); models may have seen the secure versions. The paper does not discuss contamination of its own tasks.
- [I] Claude 4 Sonnet powers the environment-building agent (p. 17) and SWE-agent drives task construction (p. 4); the best-scoring setting is SWE-agent + Claude 4 Sonnet. A construction–evaluation affinity bias is possible but untested.
- [I] The "generic" condition already contains a security reminder (p. 7, p. 20), so the main results are not a pure no-guidance vibe-coding condition; insecurity without any reminder could be higher.
- [I] Security is checked by few tests per task (mean 3.5, p. 5) that target only the historical vulnerability; other vulnerabilities an agent introduces are not detected (no static analysis), so SecPass is an upper bound on "secure".
- [I] The "most secure LLM / framework" ranking (SecPass ⊥ FuncPass, p. 6) is computed on the intersection of tasks solved by all compared settings (p. 6). Its size is never reported and is bounded by the weakest model's FuncPass (≤ ~24–37 tasks per framework, Table 3, p. 7), and solved-by-all tasks are likely the easiest. Differences such as 30.4% vs 18.3% may therefore rest on a handful of tasks.
- [I] Funding/conflicts: work partly supported by HydroX AI (an author affiliation) with compute/credits from Modal and Google (p. 9); Gemini models are evaluated. No visible bias (Gemini 2.5 Pro scores lowest functionally), noted per the appraisal checklist.
## 12. Relevance to our research
- Research questions addressed (RQ ids): RQ2 (functional correctness measured with human-written tests), RQ3 (security as a quality dimension of agent/vibe-coded code), RQ4 (test-oracle design, human verification of tests, single-run pass@1), RQ5 (functional–quality divergence; no maintainability measure).
- How it informs our work [I]: SusVibes shows that passing functional tests says little about a second quality dimension (security): ~80% of functionally correct patches failed the security tests (p. 6). This is direct evidence that fidelity and code quality must be measured separately. Its task design (mask an existing implementation, describe it in natural language, ask an agent to re-implement it, test against the original's tests) is structurally a reconstruction-from-specification setup that could be adapted to behaviour-based reconstruction. It does not measure maintainability, UI behaviour or whole-application rebuilds, so relevance is medium.

# PropGen: Automated Property Generation for Property-Based Testing of Mobile Apps

- Authors: Xiong, Yiheng; Song, Shiwen; Ma, Bo; Su, Ting; Xie, Xiaofei
- Year / venue: 2026, Proceedings of the 41st IEEE/ACM International Conference on Automated Software Engineering (published)
- DOI: 10.1145/3832783.3837536 · URL: https://doi.org/10.1145/3832783.3837536
## 1. Research objective
- [A] Automate the construction of properties for property-based testing (PBT) of mobile apps with LLMs. A *property* here is a rule of the form precondition -> interaction -> expected postcondition that must hold whenever it applies; PBT then generates many GUI event sequences and checks whether the rule is violated (p. 1, p. 3).
- [A] Motivation: non-crashing functional bugs lack explicit test oracles; PBT can expose them, but writing properties by hand is "difficult and expensive", and apps rarely have specifications (p. 1). Asking an LLM directly for properties is unreliable because, without evidence of how the app behaves, "LLMs tend to hallucinate properties that the app never satisfies" (p. 2).
## 7. Key findings
1. PropGen inferred 1,282 functionalities, 1,210 valid (94.4%) and 977 correctly executed (76.2%), vs DroidAgent 575 inferred, 491 valid (85.4%), 187 correctly executed (32.5%) — [V] (p. 8, p. 9).
2. PropGen generated 985 properties, 912 valid (92.6%; 78.7%-98.8% per app) — [V] (p. 8). The naive LLM baseline (app description only) produced 240 properties, none valid, because it referenced non-existent widgets — [V] (p. 9).
3. 127 properties caused false positives during PBT; 118 (92.9%) were successfully refined, mostly by changing the postcondition (64) or precondition (53), only 1 by changing the interaction; of 9 failures, 5 were misdiagnoses and 4 drifted from the original intent — [V] (p. 9).
4. Properties found 25 previously unknown functional bugs in the latest app versions; 5 fixed by developers at writing time; categories: functionality does not take effect (12), state inconsistency (6), unexpected wrong behaviour (4), navigation failure (3) — [V] (p. 9-10).
5. Of the 25 bugs, only 7 (28%) were in the detection scope of the four prior tools and 3 (12%) were rediscovered; the prior tools together found 13 unknown bugs, of which PropGen missed 10 — [V] (p. 10). [A] The approaches are "complementary rather than substitutable" (p. 10).
6. Cost: about $14.86 and 6,364k tokens per app for PropGen vs $13.01 and 5,109k tokens for DroidAgent; refinement about $0.02 per property — [V] (p. 9).
## 9. Limitations we identified
- [I] **Not fully automatic.** Refinement is triggered only after a human confirms a violation is a false positive (p. 4, p. 6), and bug confirmation is manual (p. 8). The cost of this human effort is not reported.
- [I] **No repeated runs with temperature 1.0.** A single run is stated for the RQ4 prior techniques (p. 8). For PropGen's RQ1-RQ3 no repetition is reported. Decoding is stochastic (p. 7), so it is unknown how stable the counts are.
- [I] **Weak naive baseline.** The naive baseline sees only the app's description, never the GUI (p. 7). Its 0/240 result shows that runtime evidence is needed, but does not test simpler runtime-grounded alternatives.
- [I] **Author-tool overlap.** Ting Su (PropGen co-author) co-authored Genie, Odin, PBFDroid, Kea and Kea2. Xiong co-authored Genie, Kea and Kea2, and Bo Ma co-authored Kea2 (refs [29], [51], [52], [63], [71], p. 12-13). So three of the four RQ4 baselines (Genie, Odin, PBFDroid, p. 7) come from the PropGen authors' own group. The scope analysis is manual (p. 8). Its validation by "the authors of Genie, Odin, and PBFDroid" (p. 8) involves the same group. The PBFDroid properties were written by the PropGen authors (p. 8). Only VisionDroid (p. 7) is independent. This is not necessarily bias in PropGen's favour, since the group knows these tools well. But the scope analysis is not independently validated.
- [I] **Annotator blinding and kappa scope not reported.** Whether the annotators were blind to which tool (PropGen or DroidAgent) produced each functionality is not reported (p. 7). The set of items over which Cohen's kappa was computed (PropGen only, or both tools) is not stated (p. 7).
- [I] **Contamination check is partial.** "Not previously reported" bugs (p. 11) do not show that the model lacks prior knowledge of app behaviour; app functionality, not bugs, is what the model may have memorised.
- [I] **Validity is judged against human understanding, not a specification** (p. 7). This is reasonable but means "valid" depends on annotators' familiarisation of at least fifteen minutes per app (p. 7).
## 12. Relevance to our research
- Research questions addressed (RQ ids): RQ2, RQ4, RQ5 (indirect); RQ1 only tangentially. Not RQ3.
- How it informs our work [I]: PropGen does not generate or reconstruct applications, so it gives no direct evidence on fidelity or code quality of AI-built apps. Its value is as a method. It recovers behavioural specifications from a running app without source code or written specifications. It does this through runtime observation plus light static analysis of the APK: manifest activity names (p. 4) and a string whitelist (p. 5). So it is grey-box rather than purely observational. The authors call this "exploring a black-box app" [A] (p. 11). This is exactly the kind of oracle a reverse-engineering study needs to check functional fidelity (RQ2, RQ5). Its result that an LLM without runtime observation produces 0 valid properties (p. 9) supports grounding any reconstruction or test generation in observed behaviour. Its evaluation design (independent annotators with kappa, scope analysis for oracle-dependent tools) is a good template for RQ4. Relevance is therefore medium.
=========== chen2025iwr
# IWR-Bench: Can LVLMs reconstruct interactive webpage from a user interaction video?

- Authors: Chen, Yang; Liu, Minghao; Shen, Yufan; Li, Yunwen; Huang, Tianyuan; Fang, Xinyu
- Year / venue: 2025, arXiv (preprint)
- DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2509.24709 · URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.24709
## 1. Research objective
- [A] Introduce and formalise Interactive Webpage Reconstruction (IWR): given a video of a user interacting with a webpage plus the page's static assets, a model must generate code that reproduces both the visual appearance and the interactive behaviour seen in the video (p. 4, p. 5).
- [A] Motivation: existing webpage-to-code benchmarks are static (screenshot-to-code, e.g. Design2Code, WebSight) or treat interactions as single-step, stateless events (Interaction2Code), and do not provide the static assets needed for a realistic rebuild (p. 2, p. 6).
## 7. Key findings
1. Interactive reconstruction from video is far from solved: GPT-5 is best with Final 36.35, IFS 24.39, VFS 64.25; no model exceeds 25 IFS — [V] (p. 8, p. 9).
2. IFS is below VFS for every model: e.g. Claude-Sonnet-4 VFS 61.34 vs IFS 22.29; GPT-4o (latest) VFS 57.55 vs IFS 17.55 — [V] (p. 8, p. 9). [I] Caveat: the two scores do not share a denominator. VFS is computed only over checkpoints that were successfully reached (p. 7), IFS over every action in the trajectory (p. 7), so part of the gap is built into the metric definitions (see §9). The low absolute IFS (finding 1) does not depend on this caveat.
3. Hierarchy: the top eight models by Final Score are all proprietary (GPT-5 36.35 down to GPT-4.1 31.99); proprietary models span Final 16.50-36.35; the best open-source model, Qwen3-VL (thinking), scores 31.15 and is above 9 of the 17 proprietary models; video-specialised models are lowest (VideoLLaMA3-7B 13.67, InternVideo-2.5-Chat-8B 10.07) — [V] (p. 8, p. 9). So the proprietary and open-source ranges overlap. [A] Authors conclude general multimodal reasoning and code generation matter more than video-specialised architectures (p. 9).
4. Thinking variants raise IFS and Final slightly in all three pairs: Claude-Sonnet-4 Final 34.62 vs 34.00 (IFS 23.65 vs 22.29); Claude-Opus-4 34.13 vs 33.33 (IFS 23.61 vs 21.83); Gemini-2.5-Pro 30.36 vs 30.31 (IFS 21.65 vs 20.51). But they lower VFS in all three pairs: 60.20 vs 61.34; 58.67 vs 60.18; 50.67 vs 53.18 — [V] (p. 8, p. 9). [A] The authors describe the gains as "Consistent but Moderate" (p. 9); this goes beyond the data, given the VFS drops and the Gemini Final difference of 0.05 points.
5. Final Score drops sharply from static L1 tasks to interactive ones: GPT-5 61.85 on L1 vs 35.43 / 35.12 / 25.26 on L2 / L3 / L4 — [V] (p. 17). L1 is the highest category for every model, but L2 and L3 are similar and their order varies by model (e.g. Claude-Sonnet-4 31.04 vs 32.86; Claude-Opus-4 (thinking) 31.52 vs 32.57; GPT-4.1 30.32 vs 31.98) — [V] (p. 17). By visual category, Final Score is lowest on data-dense layouts (GPT-5 26.05 on V4 vs 44.77 on V1), with V3 above V2 (43.77 vs 30.73) — [V] (p. 18). By domain, Life & Community is the weakest and Entertainment & Media the strongest (GPT-5 22.80 vs 47.24) — [V] (p. 19). All three category tables report Final Score only, not IFS or VFS (p. 16). So they do not show whether functional or visual fidelity drives the differences. [I] The robust contrast is L1 vs the rest; strata are small (about 9 L1 and 12 L4 tasks, see §9).
6. Judge reliability: three PhD students watched the agent run on 100 random generated pages; agent evaluation failed in only three cases (ambiguous element descriptors), which were then fixed — [V] (p. 9).
7. Case studies show distinct failure points, e.g. a model completing filter and sort but failing "add to cart"; a model failing to merge tiles in a 2048 rebuild; missing first-step elements blocking all later actions — [V] (p. 19-21).
## 9. Limitations we identified
- [I] **IFS and VFS are not comparable in scale.** VFS averages only over checkpoints that were "successfully reached" (p. 7). It therefore cannot be lowered by functional failures that block later states. IFS divides successful actions by all actions in the trajectory (p. 7). Part of the IFS < VFS gap is therefore a property of the metrics, not only of the models. The authors mention unreachable states only to justify alpha = 0.7 (p. 7). How VFS is scored for a task where no visual checkpoint is reached is not reported. This weakens the headline "visual is solved better than function" reading. It does not weaken the finding that IFS is low in absolute terms.
- [I] **No code-quality or maintainability measurement.** The task forces a single self-contained HTML file with no libraries (p. 20), and only behaviour and appearance are scored. The benchmark therefore says nothing about code structure, readability or maintainability (our RQ3), and the single-file format is far from how real apps are built.
- [I] **Judge overlap.** Gemini-2.5-Pro is both the assertion/visual judge (p. 7) and one of the evaluated models (p. 8). Self-preference bias is not tested; its mid-table rank makes a large bias unlikely, but it is not ruled out.
- [I] **Moderate human alignment, tuned in-sample.** The weights were chosen to maximise rho on the same 60 instances used to report rho = 0.57 / 0.65 (p. 17); there is no held-out check. These are moderate correlations, so the Final Score is only partly aligned with human judgement. Agreement among the five raters is not reported (p. 17). The same is true of agreement among the three PhD students who observed the agent (p. 9).
- [I] **Evaluator re-scoring after fixes unclear.** The 3/100 agent failures were "rectified" (p. 9). Whether all 28 x 113 evaluations were re-run after the fixes is not reported. Element resolution from natural-language descriptions (p. 17) may itself involve a model, so the "deterministic" label (p. 6) is an author claim we cannot check.
- [I] **No variance reporting.** Single point estimates with default sampling settings (p. 8) and no repeated runs reported; several model gaps are tiny (e.g. 30.36 vs 30.31, p. 9) and could be noise.
- [I] **Unclear failure propagation.** It is not stated whether a failed early action stops the replay or whether later actions are still attempted (p. 7); Case 4 suggests failures cascade (p. 21). This affects how IFS should be read.
- [I] **Possible contamination.** Tasks come from popular live websites (e.g. an IMDb mock in Figure 2, p. 2); models may have seen similar code in training. Asset anonymisation (p. 5) reduces name cues but not memorised page structure. Not discussed by the authors.
- [I] **Small strata.** L1 (about 9 tasks) and L4 (about 12 tasks), derived by us from the percentages on p. 3, give unstable per-category scores.
## 12. Relevance to our research
- Research questions addressed (RQ ids): RQ1, RQ2, RQ4, RQ5 (indirectly). Not RQ3.
- How it informs our work [I]: IWR-Bench is a direct example of AI "reverse engineering" an application from observable behaviour (a video) rather than from a written spec, so it fits RQ1. Its main result is central evidence for RQ2: functional correctness is low for every model (IFS < 25), and IFS sits far below VFS. The size of that gap is partly a product of how VFS is defined (only reached checkpoints, see §9). Its metric design (action replay with an agent judge, visual composite, human-calibrated weights) and weaknesses (no variance, in-sample calibration, judge overlap) inform RQ4 and our own study design. It illustrates the gap behind RQ5: fidelity is measured, but code quality and maintainability are not. [I] The result is directionally consistent with [@bai2026recreationworld], which finds static structure reproduced better than action-conditioned behaviour within its programmatic tests [@bai2026recreationworld, p. 16-17]. However, bai's visual channel is not higher than its programmatic channel in aggregate [@bai2026recreationworld, p. 11]. The two papers measure the gap differently, and here the gap is partly metric-induced (see §9). The convergence supports "static structure is easier than interaction logic". It does not support "visual fidelity systematically exceeds functional fidelity".

# Design2Code: Benchmarking Multimodal Code Generation for Automated Front-End Engineering

- Authors: Si, Chenglei; Zhang, Yanzhe; Li, Ryan; Yang, Zhengyuan; Liu, Ruibo; Yang, Diyi
- Year / venue: 2025, Proceedings of the 2025 Conference of the Nations of the Americas Chapter of the Association for Computational Linguistics: Human Language Technologies (Volume 1: Long Papers) (published)
- DOI: 10.18653/v1/2025.naacl-long.199 · URL: https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.naacl-long.199
## 1. Research objective
- Build the first real-world benchmark for converting a webpage screenshot (visual design) directly into HTML/CSS code with multimodal LLMs (MLLMs), with automatic visual-fidelity metrics and human evaluation, and use it to compare models and prompting methods [A] (p. 1).
## 7. Key findings
1. GPT-4o is best on every automatic dimension (Direct: Block 93.0, Text 98.2, Position 85.5, Color 84.1, CLIP 90.4) [V] (p. 6); in human pairwise evaluation GPT-4o Direct wins 92% vs the Gemini baseline [V] (p. 7).
2. [A] Text-augmented prompting "increases the block-match score and text similarity score on most tested models" (p. 5). [V] Block-Match rises for 4 of 7 prompted models (e.g. GPT-4V 85.8 → 87.6; Gemini 80.2 → 84.8) but falls for GPT-4o (93.0 → 92.4), Claude 3 Opus (90.2 → 89.8) and Idefics2 (46.7 → 23.6); Text rises for all except Idefics2 (80.3 → 55.6) (Table 1, p. 6). [V] Self-revision helps GPT-4V and Claude 3 a little and does not help Gemini or open models (DeepSeek-VL self-revision collapses to Block 30.1) [V] (pp. 5–6).
3. Main weakness: models "mostly lag in recalling visual elements ... and generating correct layout designs" [A] (p. 1); on Design2Code-HARD, the authors state that state-of-the-art models "fail to generate 30% - 40% of the block elements" [A] (p. 8). Block-Match is an area-weighted share that also penalises hallucinated blocks (pp. 4–5), so it is not a count of missed elements [I]. [V] HARD Block-Match ranges 56.6–75.1 across configurations (GPT-4o Direct 56.6, i.e. more than 40% unmatched area) (Table 3, p. 8).
4. [A] Authors state that WebSight VLM-8B and Design2Code-18B "match Gemini direct prompting" (p. 6). [V] Design2Code-18B is close on Block (78.5 vs 80.2), but WebSight VLM-8B is far lower on Block (55.9) and Text (86.6 vs 94.6), though higher on CLIP (87.6 vs 84.4), Position (77.3 vs 72.3) and Color (79.4 vs 66.2) (Table 1, p. 6).
5. Direct assessment: 49% of GPT-4V self-revision pages judged interchangeable with the reference; generated pages preferred over the original in 64% of cases [V] (p. 6). Kappa for these questions is low (0.32, 0.26) [V] (p. 16).
6. Humans weight high-level layout and presence of content, not exact text: text similarity has a negative coefficient in predicting preference [V] (p. 7). Human and automatic rankings disagree in places (e.g. humans prefer WebSight VLM-8B over Design2Code-18B despite worse Block-Match/Text) [V] (pp. 6–7).
7. Total tag count is the strongest difficulty indicator (r = −0.28 with Block-Match for GPT-4V self-revision); non-English pages are harder [V] (pp. 8, 15).
8. Code quality, maintainability or functional behaviour of the generated code is not measured; all metrics are visual/textual on rendered screenshots [V] (pp. 3–4).
## 9. Limitations we identified
- [I] Fidelity is purely visual (screenshot similarity). No functional, behavioural, or code-level quality measure (e.g. HTML validity, semantic structure, maintainability, responsiveness) — based on metric definitions pp. 3–4. For our project this means Design2Code says nothing about whether a rebuilt page works or is maintainable.
- [I] JavaScript, iframes, SVG and external links are stripped and prompts say no JS is needed (pp. 13–14); the task is reconstruction of a static, de-functionalised snapshot, not of an application.
- [I] Human validation is modest: 100 pages, Fleiss' κ only 0.46/0.32/0.26 (p. 16), logistic regression accuracy 79.9% on a 50-page test half (p. 7). The very high Pearson r = 0.975 is computed over only 10 system-level points (p. 17) and compares predicted wins with human "win + tie" — an aggregation that inflates agreement.
- [I] Test pages come from C4, a common pretraining corpus; data contamination is not discussed (grep for "contamin" finds nothing).
- [I] Single run per configuration with greedy decoding; no variance or significance testing between models (pp. 5–6, 14).
- [I] Design2Code-HARD pages are sourced from GitHub (p. 14) and filtered "using the same filtering process" (p. 8); how the GitHub pages were found and selected is not described, so the HARD set's representativeness cannot be judged.
- [I] The metric-validation agreement is not out-of-sample. The logistic regression is trained on 50 of the 100 human-annotated pages (p. 7); the linear model for simulated win rates is refit "on the same training examples" and applied to "all 484 examples", which include those training pages (p. 17); and the human "win + tie" rates it is compared with come from the same 100 pages (Table 8, p. 17). So r = 0.975 / τ = 0.931 partly reflects fit on the training data, on top of being computed over only 10 system-level points.
- [I] Element metrics are text-anchored (blocks come from text segments, p. 13); images, icons and non-text elements are only covered by CLIP.
## 12. Relevance to our research
- Research questions addressed (RQ ids): RQ1, RQ2, RQ4, RQ5 (RQ3 only by absence).
- How it informs our work [I]: Design2Code is the reference benchmark for screenshot-to-code reconstruction and the origin of the Block/Text/Position/Color/CLIP metric family reused by later work. It shows how visual fidelity can be measured and partly validated against humans, and it clearly leaves out interaction, functionality and code quality — the dimensions our project needs.

- Authors: He, Hao; Miller, Courtney; Agarwal, Shyam; Kästner, Christian; Vasilescu, Bogdan
- Year / venue: 2026, Proceedings of the 23rd International Conference on Mining Software Repositories (published)
- DOI: 10.1145/3793302.3793349 · URL: https://doi.org/10.1145/3793302.3793349
## 1. Research objective
Estimate the causal effect of adopting an LLM agent assistant (Cursor) on project-level development velocity and software quality in open-source GitHub projects, and test how velocity and quality interact over time [A] (p. 1, p. 2).
## 7. Key findings
1. Main DiD (Borusyak): lines added +28.58% (±13.7%, p < 0.05); commits +2.63% (n.s.); static analysis warnings +30.26% (±6.66%, p < 0.001); duplicated line density +7.03% (n.s.); code complexity +41.64% (±7.62%, p < 0.001) — [V] (p. 7). Significance stars were read from the PDF image; the text extraction attaches *** to every cell.
2. The velocity gain is transient: only months 1–2 are significant; commits +55.4% / +14.5% and lines added +281.3% / +48.4% in months 1 / 2 — [V] (p. 8, Figure 3).
3. Quality effects persist to month 6 for warnings and complexity; all outcomes pass the pre-trend tests — [V] (p. 8, Figure 3).
4. Panel GMM: velocity does not significantly raise warnings or complexity; Cursor has no significant direct effect on warnings (−0.011) once codebase size is controlled; Cursor still raises complexity (0.086, p < 0.01, reported as a "9% baseline increase") — [V] (p. 8, Table 3).
5. Panel GMM: a 100% increase in complexity or warnings goes with a 64.5% / 50.3% decrease in next-month lines added; Cursor gives a 1.84x baseline increase in lines added; ~3x complexity or ~5x warnings would cancel the gain — [V] (p. 8).
6. Robustness: quality effects are stronger in high-usage subsets (contributors who touched Cursor files made ≥80% of commits; months with config edits); they persist in very active repos and with/without traces of other AI tools (382 treated repos show other tools, 345 of them Copilot) — [V] (p. 9, p. 10, Figure 4).
7. Estimator sensitivity: TWFE agrees (warnings +18.48%, complexity +26.04%, both p < 0.001), but Callaway & Sant'Anna gives negative, non-significant quality estimates (warnings −10.49%, complexity −3.80%); the authors conclude the quality findings need caution — [V] (p. 16, Table 6).
8. Descriptive warning analysis (convenience sample, 195,010 warnings from 933 rules, 20-category taxonomy): 16/20 categories rise post-adoption; largest Naming Conventions +21.32, Code Hygiene +16.15, Code Complexity +15.34, Code Style +14.76 new warnings per repo per month; Logic Error +2.09, Security +1.98; API Usage −4.35 — [V] (p. 18, Table 8).
9. The authors interpret that much of the warning increase runs through velocity → codebase size → technical debt, so Cursor "may not necessarily introduce more code quality issues than non-adopting projects moving with the same velocity", whereas complexity is inherently higher in Cursor code — [A] (p. 10).
## 9. Limitations we identified
- [I] Internal inconsistencies in reported numbers: the discussion cites a "25.1%" complexity increase attributed to Table 2 (p. 10) and a "25%" increase attributed to Table 3 (p. 11), while Table 2 reports +41.64% and Table 3 a 0.086 coefficient (~9%) (p. 7, p. 8). Probably left over from an earlier version; use the table values.
- [I] Sample counts differ between text and tables: 806 treated and 1,380 controls (p. 2, p. 4) versus 801 treated and 1,172 controls in Table 7 (p. 17). The paper does not explain the difference. Also, with 1:3 nearest-neighbour matching (p. 5), 806 treated repos would need up to 2,418 controls, yet only 1,380 distinct controls are reported (p. 2), so controls were shared across treated units or cohorts. The paper hints at matching with replacement ("many adopters matched the same control during 1:1 matching", p. 5) but does not state it, and does not say how reused controls are weighted or how this affects the independence of observations.
- [I] Warnings and complexity are repository totals, so they scale with codebase size; in the GMM, lines of code absorbs most variance and removes the direct Cursor effect on warnings (p. 8). "+30% warnings" is therefore mainly "more code", not a higher warning density. For RQ3 the per-code quality signal is complexity, not warnings. Based on Table 3 (p. 8).
- [I] The headline quality result depends on the estimator (Borusyak/TWFE positive; Callaway–Sant'Anna negative and n.s.) (p. 16); the abstract does not mention this.
- [I] Cursor-generated code is not identified at file or line level; quality changes are attributed to whole repositories, including human-written code.
- [I] The warning taxonomy was produced by the first author together with Claude Opus 4.5 (p. 18); no inter-rater or validation check is reported.
- [I] No functional-correctness or test outcome is measured; quality is static analysis only.

- Research questions addressed (RQ ids): RQ3 (main), RQ4, RQ5.
- How it informs our work [I]: the strongest real-world causal evidence in our set that agentic, vibe-coding-style development raises code complexity and static-analysis warnings at project level. It does not study reconstruction from UI/behaviour, so it informs RQ3 by analogy only. # Understanding the Code and Architectural Smells Generated by LLMs and Autonomous Agents

- Authors: Zhu, Yuecai; Tsantalis, Nikolaos; Rigby, Peter C.
- Year / venue: 2026, Proceedings of the 41st IEEE/ACM International Conference on Automated Software Engineering (published)
- DOI: 10.1145/3832783.3834413 · URL: https://doi.org/10.1145/3832783.3834413
## 1. Research objective
Audit the code smells (technical debt) in code produced by single LLMs and by a multi-agent system, and analyse how moving from single-file algorithmic tasks to multi-file applications changes the density and kinds of smells [A] (p. 1).
## 7. Key findings
1. Exp I: Long Method counts were higher for larger LLMs than for humans — zero-shot Qwen Coder 480B 11, Qwen Coder 30B 7, Gemini 2.5 Pro 5, deepseek 2, Llama 3 70B 0, human 1 (across 90 problems) — [V] (p. 7, Table 1).
2. Exp I: Temporal Field appeared only in human solutions (4 instances), never in LLM output — [V] (p. 6, p. 7).
3. Exp I: few-shot prompting did not reduce Long Method (Qwen 480B 11 → 13; Gemini 5 → 8) — [V] (p. 6, p. 7, Table 2).
4. Exp I interpretation: a "Reasoning-Complexity Trade-off" — more capable models produce more procedural bloat; larger models' failed solutions had more Long Methods — [A] (p. 6, p. 7). The failed/passing split is not shown in any table.
5. Exp II: ranked by mean count over all Exp II repositories (pooled), the most prevalent smells were Too Many Branches, High Response for a Class, Potential Improper API Usage, Scattered Functionality and Unstable Dependency — [V] (p. 8, Figure 1 caption; p. 7). The authors add that these smells appear consistently "across all stages and scenarios" — [A] (p. 7); Figure 1 pools repositories and no per-stage or per-scenario breakdown is shown.
6. Exp II: TLoC correlated with code-level (ρ = 0.53, p = 0.017), structural (ρ = 0.59, p = 0.007) and architectural smell counts (ρ = 0.94, p < 0.001); ToF with architectural smells ρ = 0.72 (p < 0.001) — [V] (p. 8, p. 9, Table 3).
7. Exp II: scenario affected architectural smells (H = 9.86, p = 0.043); requirement stage had no significant effect on any smell level (p > 0.8) — [V] (p. 8, p. 9, Table 3).
8. Authors' framing: a "Volume-Quality Inverse Law" and a "Modular Mirage" (files are separated but behaviour is fragmented); agents act like "junior developers" lacking architectural foresight — [A] (p. 8, p. 9).
9. Abstract/introduction claim that functional correctness is decoupled from quality ("running code is just as likely to be structurally flawed as failed code") — [A] (p. 1, p. 2). No supporting data is reported in the results.
## 9. Limitations we identified
- [I] Central claims rest on very small counts. Exp I differences are a handful of smell instances over 90 files (e.g. 11 vs 1 Long Method), with no test, no per-LoC normalisation and no variance across runs (p. 7). Treat as descriptive.
- [I] The "Volume-Quality Inverse Law" correlates smell *counts* with code size (p. 8, p. 9). More code mechanically yields more smell instances, so ρ = 0.94 does not show that larger code is worse per line. The paper says size metrics allow density normalisation (p. 5) but reports no density analysis.
- [I] The claims that functional correctness does not mitigate decay (p. 1, p. 2) are not backed by any reported correctness measure; Exp II apps were not executed or tested at all as far as the paper reports.
- [I] The "longitudinal"/"architectural rot" framing (p. 2, p. 5) is questionable: the four stage prompts are self-contained (p. 5), and the paper does not say that each stage evolves the previous codebase. It may be four independent generations.
- [I] Exp II has n ≈ 20, one LLM backend, one framework, no human or single-agent baseline, and 15 significance tests without correction (p. 9); the "near-perfect predictor" wording overstates a single small sample.
- [I] Internal inconsistencies: RQ numbering differs between introduction and results; "random" vs "stratified" sampling; "ALpF" undefined; smell abbreviations in Exp I (EC, HFI) are not expanded (p. 1, p. 4, p. 6, p. 7, p. 9).
- [I] PyExamine validity: two smell types were dropped as false positives after an unquantified audit (p. 6); no precision figures for the architectural smells that drive the main conclusion.
- [I] Model inference settings (temperature, quantisation; model names suggest local deployment) are not reported, which limits reproducibility.
## 12. Relevance to our research
- Research questions addressed (RQ ids): RQ3 (main), RQ1, RQ4.
- How it informs our work [I]: Exp II is close to our setting: complete small applications (games, GUI tools, web backend) generated by an agent from natural-language requirements. It gives a smell taxonomy for agent-built apps (God-class branching, unstable dependencies, scattered functionality) that a reconstruction study could measure. Its evidence is weak (preprint version, tiny n, count-based statistics, no correctness data), so it should support hypotheses rather than conclusions, and it illustrates RQ4 weaknesses (no size normalisation, no baselines).
