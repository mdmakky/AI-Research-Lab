## SUMMARY PER KEY

**li2026llm** · ready · full-text (arXiv 2601.19239v2, 25 Sep 2026)
- Compares 5 specialized LLM detectors (RepoAudit, KNighter, IRIS, LLMDFA, INFERROI) and 2 general coding agents (Codex/GPT-5.4, Claude Code/Sonnet 4.6) with CodeQL, Semgrep, SpotBugs and Clang Static Analyzer (CSA). Test sets: 265 known C/C++ and Java vulnerabilities (8 CWEs) and 24 real-world projects.
- The general agents have the highest recall (59.55% C/C++, 64–82% Java). Specialized tools and SAST often have near-zero recall and a 75–100% sampled false discovery rate (SFDR, the share of sampled warnings that are false). Cost reaches $1,247.66 and 225M input tokens per project.
- Surprising or doubtful:
  - Codex is both an evaluated tool and the TP/FP labeller. It also gets the lowest SFDR (9.09%), and that figure rests on only about 22 warnings.
  - "Precision" on the controlled set only counts false positives inside known vulnerable files.
  - The appendices (A–C) are not in the PDF.

**guo2025repoaudit** · ready · full-text (arXiv 2501.18160v3, 29 May 2025; its p. 1 already carries the ICML 2025 / PMLR 267 footer)
- An LLM agent that audits whole repositories (Claude 3.5 Sonnet). On 15 projects it finds 40 true positives (TP) against 11 false positives (FP), 78.43% precision, at $2.54 and 0.44 h per project. Meta Infer finds 7 TP / 2 FP; Amazon CodeGuru finds 0 TP / 18 FP.
- In nine further projects it reports 185 new bugs, 174 of them confirmed or fixed.
- Surprising or doubtful:
  - It conflicts with li2026llm on overlapping Linux and ImageMagick targets. li2026llm finds RepoAudit recall of 0–5.71% and SFDR of 92.77–95.41%.
  - Recall here only means reproducing 21 known bugs.
  - The paper doesn't say how RepoAudit's own TP/FP labels were decided.
  - Internal inconsistencies: "ten" vs "nine" repositories; the MLK column of Table 4 adds up to 75, not the printed 76.

**yildiz2025benchmarking** · ready · full-text (ACL 2025)
- Introduces JITVul: 879 CVEs from PrimeVul, 91 CWEs, 1,758 paired vulnerable/fixed functions in C/C++. It compares a plain LLM, a dependency-augmented LLM and a ReAct agent (GPT-4o / 4o-mini), each with CoT and few-shot prompting variants.
- F1 stays around 48–66% while pairwise accuracy (pAcc, both versions of a pair labelled correctly) stays at or below 20.17%. ReAct gives the best pAcc.
- Surprising or doubtful:
  - There is no SAST, deep-learning or cost comparison.
  - Every GPT-4o/4o-mini pAcc is below the 25% that random guessing would give.
  - In the appendix, DeepSeek-Coder-V2 with dependency augmentation reaches F1 82.09 / pAcc 69.24, which contradicts the "ReAct is better" conclusion for that model.
  - Several pAcc values are exact multiples of 1/119, which suggests some settings ran on about 119 pairs, not 879. This is my inference; the paper doesn't say.

**tan2026think** · ready · abstract-only (no OA full text)
- A study on PrimeVul Pair with three phases: instruct vs thinking (reasoning) model variants, single/dual/multi-agent designs, and explanation quality.
- Thinking modes raise F1 in 7 of 8 settings, mainly through recall, and use 2.7–4.4× more energy. The dual-agent design has the best F1 but flags 88.2% of functions as vulnerable.
- Sections 3–6, 8 and 10 say "full text not available". Its evidence should carry less weight. The user would need to supply the ASE 2026 PDF for a full analysis.

# LLM-based Vulnerability Detection at Project Scale: An Empirical Study

- Authors: Li, Fengjie; Jiang, Jiajun; Chen, Dongchi; Xiong, Yingfei
- Year / venue: 2026, IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering (published)
- DOI: 10.1109/tse.2026.3742453 · URL: https://doi.org/10.1109/tse.2026.3742453
- Source text: `fulltext.txt` (page refs = PDF page index). Our PDF is arXiv:2601.19239v2 (25 Sep 2026, stamp on p. 1), not the IEEE version of record; page numbers and possibly some numbers may differ from the TSE copy.

Labels: **[V]** verified in the paper's own data/tables/results · **[A]** author claim · **[I]** our interpretation.
Every [V]/[A] item ends with a page ref `(p. N)`. Write "not reported" when the paper does not say.

## 1. Research objective
Systematically evaluate specialized project-scale LLM-based vulnerability detectors against general-purpose coding agents and traditional static analyzers on (a) known vulnerabilities and (b) current real-world projects, explaining their false positives and measuring their cost [A] (p. 1, p. 2).

## 2. Research questions / hypotheses
- RQ1: How effective are LLM-based methods at detecting known vulnerabilities in a controlled benchmark? [V] (p. 3)
- RQ2: How reliable are they on real-world projects/modules, in terms of false discovery rates? [V] (p. 3)
- RQ3: What are the root causes of false positives? [V] (p. 4)
- RQ4: What is the overhead (tokens, money, time) of project-scale LLM detection? [V] (p. 4)
- No formal hypotheses.

## 3. Methodology
- Research design: comparative empirical benchmark study, plus qualitative FP root-cause taxonomy (open coding, double coding) and LLM-assisted (Codex) labelling with stratified manual validation [V] (p. 6, p. 11, p. 13).
- Approach / model / intervention (Table I, p. 4) [V]:
  - Specialized LLM-based: RepoAudit (multi-agent; Claude Sonnet 3.5 / GPT-5.4; C/C++; CWE-401/416/476), KNighter (LLM-synthesised Clang Static Analyzer checkers; o3-mini / GPT-5.4; C/C++), IRIS (LLM-inferred sources/sinks + CodeQL; GPT-4 / GPT-5.4; Java; CWE-022/078/079/094), LLMDFA (multi-agent dataflow; GPT-4 / GPT-5.4; Java; CWE-078/079), INFERROI (LLM intent inference + CFG; GPT-4 / GPT-5.4; Java; CWE-772) (p. 4, p. 5).
  - General agents: Codex (GPT-5.4) and Claude Code (Claude Sonnet 4.6), all CWEs, internet search disabled (p. 4, p. 7).
  - Each specialized method run with its original backbone and with GPT-5.4 to separate model from workflow effects (p. 4).
- Baselines or comparison conditions: traditional SAST — CodeQL (48 selected rules, C/C++ and Java), Semgrep (59 rules, C/C++ and Java), SpotBugs + Find Security Bugs (21 patterns, Java), Clang Static Analyzer CSA (12 checkers, C/C++) [V] (p. 4, p. 7). Rules were manually curated to the target CWEs; all severities treated equally [V] (p. 7). No deep-learning (fine-tuned/GNN) detectors and no prompt-only/RAG function-level methods (excluded because they do not produce repository-level warnings) [V] (p. 4). LLMxCPG excluded (needs local fine-tuning) [V] (p. 4).

## 4. Dataset and sample
- Data source(s): controlled dataset from ReposVul (C/C++), CWE-Bench-Java (Maven projects only) and JLeaks (50 randomly sampled resource leaks); all cases manually checked for validity and successful compilation [V] (p. 5). Real-world dataset: 24 actively maintained projects/modules with historical vulnerabilities in the studied CWEs (e.g. Linux subsystems, ImageMagick, Vim, gpac, bitlbee; OpenOLAT, Spark, DSpace, XStream, Jenkins, Keycloak, Tika, xwiki, ActiveMQ, RxJava, jsoup) [V] (p. 5, p. 6).
- Sample size (N) and unit: 265 known vulnerabilities — C/C++: CWE-401 50, CWE-416 25, CWE-476 35; Java: CWE-022 49, CWE-078 11, CWE-079 28, CWE-094 17, CWE-772 50 [V] (p. 6). 24 projects/modules sized 1,715 to 6,033,855 LOC [V] (p. 6). Up to 300 warnings sampled per tool per project → 6,442 warnings; 5,896 FPs classified; 355 warnings manually coded to build the taxonomy (>150 person-hours) [V] (p. 6, p. 11, p. 13).
- Sampling / selection method: for ReposVul, the three repositories with most cases per CWE (to limit build effort); 8 CWEs chosen by tool support and benchmark availability [V] (p. 5). 21 of 24 real-world commits postdate the August 2025 knowledge cutoff of GPT-5.4 and Claude Sonnet 4.6 [V] (p. 5). Several C/C++ modules come from the same Linux commit [A] (p. 18). No power analysis.
- Data availability (public? link?): artifacts (scripts, prompts, taxonomy labels, statistics) at https://github.com/Feng-Jay/LLM4Security [V] (p. 2).

## 5. Experimental setup
- Procedure, settings, hyper-parameters, tools: released replication packages of each specialized tool with hyper-parameters from the original papers; no extra timeouts or token/money budgets [V] (p. 6). Default RepoAudit configuration; IRIS/LLMDFA/INFERROI use their own LLM-generated API lists; KNighter checkers generated from 18 bug-fixing patches from its original study [V] (p. 7). General agents via official CLIs with built-in tools/skills, internet off [V] (p. 7). Official OpenAI/Anthropic APIs; local server with dual Xeon 6388, 512 GB RAM, Ubuntu 20.04.6 [V] (p. 7). Each LLM configuration run once [A] (p. 18).
- Controlled-dataset TP rule: a vulnerability counts as detected if the tool reports the labelled vulnerable program point; FPs are warnings in known vulnerable files that do not match the known data-flow pattern (IRIS protocol) [V] (p. 5, p. 6).
- Real-world labelling: Codex labels each sampled warning TP/FP with access to the repository; validation on up to 5 TPs + 5 FPs per tool per repo (197 TPs, 424 FPs) [V] (p. 11).
- Code availability: yes, project homepage above [V] (p. 2). Appendices A–C (FP examples, rule lists, detailed cost statistics) are referenced but are not in our 20-page PDF (p. 7, p. 13, p. 15).

## 6. Evaluation metrics
- Metric — what it measures — why chosen:
  - Recall = TP/(TP+FN) and Precision = TP/(TP+FP) on the controlled set, following prior studies [V] (p. 5, p. 6).
  - #Reports, #Files, #Sampled FPs and SFDR = #Sampled FPs / #Sampled Reports on real-world projects, because ground truth is unknown there [V] (p. 6).
  - Input/output tokens, USD and end-to-end minutes per project/module; cost and minutes per warning [V] (p. 6, p. 15, p. 16).
- Statistical testing (tests used, significance, effect sizes, confidence intervals): no significance tests between tools. Labelling accuracy estimated with stratified sampling, normal approximation and finite-population correction at the 95% level [V] (p. 11, p. 13); the intervals themselves are not printed in the text we have.

## 7. Key findings
1. C/C++ controlled set: general agents average recall 59.55% (131/220) and precision 32.19% (131/407); KNighter detects 0 vulnerabilities in all three CWEs; RepoAudit (GPT-5.4) detects 2 (CWE-476 recall 5.71%) — [V] (p. 7, Table IV).
2. C/C++ per CWE (Table IV): CWE-401 Codex R 68.00% / P 41.98%, Claude Code R 80.00% / P 40.00%, CodeQL R 6.00% / P 14.29%, Semgrep and CSA 0%; CWE-416 Codex R 40.00%, Claude Code R 36.00%, CodeQL/Semgrep/CSA 0%; CWE-476 Codex R 62.86%, Claude Code R 45.71%, CodeQL R 34.29% with 1,110 warnings (P 1.08%) — [V] (p. 7).
3. Java controlled set: Codex average recall 63.87% (99/155), Claude Code 81.94% (127/155); CodeQL 14.19%, Semgrep 6.67%, SpotBugs 14.84%; LLMDFA (GPT-5.4) finds 1 vulnerability; INFERROI (GPT-5.4) reaches 66.00% recall on CWE-772 vs CodeQL 10.00% and SpotBugs 6.00% — [V] (p. 9, Table VI p. 10).
4. Java precision is low for most tools (e.g. CWE-022: IRIS GPT-4 6.30%, Semgrep 0.29% from 2,075 warnings, SpotBugs 1.76%, Codex 58.73%, Claude Code 37.29%) — [V] (p. 10, Table VI).
5. Swapping in GPT-5.4 barely changes recall of specialized methods but raises some precision (IRIS CWE-078 6.06% → 20.00%; INFERROI 15.74% → 20.50%); authors conclude workflow, not model, limits recall — [V]/[A] (p. 9).
6. Incomplete API modelling and incomplete candidate exploration explain 108/108 (RepoAudit GPT-5.4), 97/110 (KNighter GPT-5.4), 40/44 (Codex) and 40/45 (Claude Code) missed C/C++ vulnerabilities — [V] (p. 7). With perfect allocation/deallocation API specs on CWE-401, recall rises: RepoAudit 0 → 48%, Codex 68 → 90%, Claude Code 80 → 96%, CodeQL 6 → 56% — [V] (p. 8, Table V).
7. Missed vulnerabilities for general agents sit in larger files (Fig. 4/6); IRIS and INFERROI are largely unaffected because they reason over compact representations — [V]/[A] (p. 9, p. 10).
8. LLM-based tools contribute most unique detections; traditional tools add few unique ones (CodeQL at most 3 unique on CWE-022) — [V] (p. 9, p. 10, Fig. 5).
9. Real-world C/C++ (Table VII): overall SFDR RepoAudit (Sonnet 3.5) 92.77%, KNighter (o3-mini) 81.25%, Codex 9.09% (2.44 warnings/project), Claude Code 74.07%, CodeQL 86.01% (752.67 warnings/project; 6,345 on gpac), Semgrep 100.00%, CSA 85.51% — [V] (p. 11). GPT-5.4 versions: RepoAudit 95.41%, KNighter 95.56% (Table VIII) — [V] (p. 11).
10. Real-world Java (Table IX): IRIS (GPT-4) 97.80% SFDR with 232.42 warnings/project, LLMDFA 100.00%, INFERROI 96.40%, Codex 31.82% (1.47 warnings/project), Claude Code 81.82%, CodeQL 77.00%, Semgrep 92.71%, SpotBugs 86.21%; traditional tools emit warnings in only about half of projects — [V] (p. 12). IRIS with GPT-5.4 drops to 78.73% (Table X) — [V] (p. 12).
11. Codex labels were 94.36% accurate on 621 manually validated warnings (population-weighted 98.16%); Codex FP-reason labels 87.76% accurate on 425 cases (weighted 92.52%) — [V] (p. 11, p. 13).
12. FP root causes: incorrect program-point classification B1 = 63.33% (3,734/5,896) and truncated interprocedural context A1 = 14.60% (861/5,896); LLM tools additionally show incorrect control flow (A2), missed sanitizers/guards (D1, >15% in 7 of 12 LLM settings) and prompt deviations (C1) — [V] (p. 15, Table XI p. 14). CodeQL's largest FP causes are A1 35.80% and path-insensitivity D3 20.17% — [V] (p. 14).
13. Cost (Table XII): RepoAudit (Sonnet 3.5) up to 225,493.97K input and 38,078.26K output tokens and $1,247.66 for one project, average $172.77; LLMDFA (GPT-4) average $61.60 and 2,000 min per Java project (max 4,638 min); IRIS (GPT-4) average $2.59, 213.44 min; INFERROI average $11.62–$12.99; KNighter $0.34–$2.36; Codex $5.38 / 14.80 min; Claude Code $9.67 / 13.19 min per project. SAST average runtimes 0.64 (Semgrep) to 60.92 (CSA) minutes, no token cost — [V] (p. 16).
14. Per warning: Codex $2.93 and 8.1 min, Claude Code $1.77 and 2.4 min; LLMDFA >$24 and >800 min per warning — [V] (p. 15, p. 16).

## 8. Limitations stated by the authors
- [A] Controlled evaluation counts warnings about other, unlabelled genuine vulnerabilities as FPs (p. 18).
- [A] Each LLM configuration run once; nondeterminism may affect results (p. 18).
- [A] Taxonomy and labels involve expert judgement; large-scale labels partly come from Codex and may contain model bias (p. 18).
- [A] Pretraining contamination possible for both datasets; post-cutoff commits may retain older code; sessions showed no explicit reliance on CVE data (p. 18).
- [A] Tool selection, two languages (C/C++, Java), 8 CWEs; several Linux modules share one repository/commit; no concurrency or type-confusion bugs; may not generalise to Rust, Go, TypeScript or multi-language projects (p. 18).

## 9. Limitations we identified
- [I] Codex (GPT-5.4) is both an evaluated detector and the main TP/FP labeller and FP-reason classifier. Codex has by far the lowest SFDR (9.09% C/C++, 31.82% Java). Validation was stratified (≤5 TPs + ≤5 FPs per tool per repo), so a judge that favours its own reports is only partly ruled out; per-tool labelling accuracy is not reported. Based on p. 11 and Tables VII/IX.
- [I] The low SFDRs of general agents rest on very few warnings (Codex: 2.44 × 9 ≈ 22 C/C++ and 1.47 × 15 ≈ 22 Java warnings), so they are imprecise; no per-tool confidence intervals are shown. Based on Tables VII and IX (p. 11, p. 12).
- [I] Controlled-set precision only counts FPs inside known vulnerable files, so it is not project-wide precision and is not comparable with SFDR or with precision in other papers. Based on p. 6.
- [I] SAST rule sets were curated by the authors (48/59/21/12 rules); other rule choices could change SAST recall and FP counts. Based on p. 7.
- [I] The controlled benchmark uses public CVEs that likely predate model training; general agents' high controlled recall may partly reflect memorisation even without explicit lookup. The post-cutoff real-world set gives no recall, so recall on unseen code is not measured. Based on p. 5, p. 18.
- [I] No deep-learning (fine-tuned) detectors are compared: informs LLM vs SAST, not LLM vs DL. Based on p. 4.
- [I] Backbones differ across tools (Claude Sonnet 4.6 vs GPT-5.4 vs GPT-4), so agent-vs-specialized comparisons mix workflow and model effects, partly controlled by the GPT-5.4 re-runs. Based on Table I (p. 4).

## 10. Threats to validity
- Internal (confounds, leakage, selection): LLM self-labelling (see 9); pretraining contamination acknowledged (p. 18); single runs (p. 18); different backbones per tool (p. 4).
- External (generalisability of sample/context): C/C++ and Java only, 8 CWEs, 24 projects with several from one Linux commit (p. 18); tools limited to publicly available, runnable ones (p. 4).
- Construct (do metrics measure the concept?): SFDR measures false alarms among sampled warnings, not missed vulnerabilities; controlled precision is file-restricted (p. 6); all SAST severities counted equally (p. 7).
- Statistical conclusion (power, multiple comparisons, variance): no significance tests between tools; small warning counts for some tools; no run-to-run variance.
- Reproducibility (data/code/setup reported?): good — artifacts, prompts and rule lists released (p. 2, p. 7); API model versions may change.

## 11. Future research directions
- Stated by authors [A]: combine flexible agents with program-analysis support such as path constraints, IRs and validators (p. 17); integrate LLMs with formal analysis/symbolic execution (p. 17); evidence-grounded API-role/specification inference with iterative validation (p. 17); explicit modelling of ownership, sanitizers, error paths and resource lifecycles (p. 17); structured multi-agent or self-verification for prompt compliance (p. 17); cost-aware designs — hierarchical/incremental analysis, caching, adaptive LLM invocation, cheaper models (p. 18); broader languages and CWE classes (p. 18).
- Suggested by us [I]: an independent (non-evaluated) judge or full manual labelling for warning triage; repeated runs with variance; LLM vs DL vs SAST on the same post-cutoff projects with recall measured via newly disclosed vulnerabilities.

## 12. Relevance to our research
- Research questions addressed (RQ ids): RQ1, RQ2, RQ3, RQ4, RQ5.
- How it informs our work [I]: one of the few head-to-head project-scale comparisons of LLM detectors and SAST (CodeQL, Semgrep, SpotBugs, CSA) on the same targets, with recall, precision/SFDR and cost. It shows "LLM vs SAST" depends heavily on the LLM workflow: general agents beat SAST on recall, specialized hybrids (IRIS, INFERROI, KNighter) often do not. Gives concrete cost data for RQ3 and an FP taxonomy shared by both families for RQ5.

# RepoAudit: An Autonomous LLM-Agent for Repository-Level Code Auditing

- Authors: Guo, Jinyao; Wang, Chengpeng; Xu, Xiangzhe; Su, Zian; Zhang, Xiangyu
- Year / venue: 2025, arXiv (preprint)
- DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2501.18160 · URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.18160
- Source text: `fulltext.txt` (page refs = PDF page index). Our PDF is arXiv:2501.18160v3 (29 May 2025). Its p. 1 already carries the footer "Proceedings of the 42nd International Conference on Machine Learning, Vancouver, Canada. PMLR 267, 2025" (p. 1), so it appears to be the camera-ready text, but rlab records the paper as an arXiv preprint.

Every [V]/[A] item ends with a page ref `(p. N)`. Write "not reported" when the paper does not say.

## 1. Research objective
Build and evaluate an LLM agent that audits whole C/C++ repositories for path-sensitive memory bugs without compilation, overcoming context limits, hallucination and cost of direct prompting [A] (p. 1, p. 2).

## 2. Research questions / hypotheses
- No explicit RQs. Implicit questions: effectiveness on known bugs, comparison with LLM-based and industrial detectors, contribution of components (ablation), sensitivity to model and temperature, real-world impact [V] (p. 7, p. 8).
- Underlying claim: LLMs are good at function-local abstraction, pointer handling and feasible-path exploration, but poor at whole-repository reasoning [A] (p. 3, p. 4).

## 3. Methodology
- Research design: design-and-evaluate tool paper with comparative experiments, ablations and a field deployment [V] (p. 7, p. 8, p. 15).
- Approach / model / intervention: three components [V] (p. 5–7): an initiator (tree-sitter pattern matchers find source values, e.g. NULL literals); an explorer that prompts the LLM one function at a time to derive data-flow facts along feasible paths, stores them in agent memory (also used as a cache) and moves to callers/callees on demand; a validator that checks control-flow order of data-flow facts with a parser and asks the LLM whether inter-procedural path conditions are satisfiable. Calling-context bound K = 4 functions, temperature 0.0, default model Claude 3.5 Sonnet [V] (p. 7).
- Baselines or comparison conditions: Meta Infer and Amazon CodeGuru (industrial static analyzers) [V] (p. 8, p. 14); single-function and multiple-function few-shot CoT prompting and the LLMDFA agent (only on functions related to RepoAudit's TPs, or reachable from sources) [V] (p. 13). No open-source SAST such as CodeQL/Semgrep/CSA, no deep-learning detectors.

## 4. Dataset and sample
- Data source(s): 15 projects (5 per bug type) taken from bug reports of prior papers (Huang et al. 2024; Shi et al. 2021, 2018) [V] (p. 7). NPD: sofa-pbrpc, ImageMagick/MagickCore, coturn, libfreenect, openldap; MLK: libsass, memcached, linux/driver/net, linux/sound, linux/mm; UAF: Redis, linux/drivers/peci, shadowsocks-libev, wabt-tool, icu i18n; 2,130–1,378,262 LoC [V] (p. 7, Table 1).
- Sample size (N) and unit: 15 projects with 21 previously reported bugs (counted as "Old" TPs) [V] (p. 8, p. 17); 9 further projects of 14K–1.7M LoC (avg 420K LoC) for the field study [V] (p. 8).
- Sampling / selection method: "well-maintained" projects from prior bug reports [A] (p. 7). The paper says both "ten actively maintained repositories" (p. 2) and "nine open-source GitHub projects" (p. 8, p. 9); Table 4 lists nine [V] (p. 9).
- Data availability (public? link?): code at https://github.com/PurCL/RepoAudit [V] (p. 1). A labelled dataset release is not reported.

## 5. Experimental setup
- Procedure, settings, hyper-parameters, tools: tree-sitter for parsing/call graphs; Claude 3.5 Sonnet at temperature 0; K = 4 [V] (p. 7). Additional runs with DeepSeek R1, Claude 3.7 Sonnet and OpenAI o3-mini, and temperatures 0.25–1.0 [V] (p. 16, p. 17). Infer tried in v1.2.0, v1.0.0 and v0.9.0; 2 projects failed to build and 5 crashed or were incompatible [V] (p. 15). Infer and CodeGuru reports were manually labelled TP/FP [V] (p. 14). How RepoAudit's own reports were judged TP or FP (who labelled, criteria, agreement) is not reported.
- Code availability: yes (p. 1).

## 6. Evaluation metrics
- Metric — what it measures — why chosen: TP, FP, precision = TP/(TP+FP); "recall" is the share of the 21 previously reported bugs reproduced (p. 17); prompting rounds, input/output tokens, USD and seconds per project (p. 8). Overall recall against all bugs in the projects is not measured.
- Statistical testing (tests used, significance, effect sizes, confidence intervals): none; single run per setting (temperature study gives one run per temperature) [V] (p. 17).

## 7. Key findings
1. RepoAudit (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) reproduces all 21 known bugs and finds 19 new ones (14 fixed in the latest commit): 40 TPs, 11 FPs, precision 78.43%; 21 TPs are inter-procedural — [V] (p. 7, Table 2 p. 8).
2. Cost: average 0.44 h (1,577.22 s), 100.67 prompts, 614,625.73 input and 46,153.07 output tokens, $2.54 per project, $0.95 per true bug; most expensive project (icu) $18.15 and 10,661.98 s — [V] (p. 7, p. 8).
3. Meta Infer: 13 projects build but 5 crash; on 8 analysed projects 7 TPs and 2 FPs, no MLK or UAF bug found — [V] (p. 8, p. 15). Amazon CodeGuru (NPD and UAF only, 10 projects): 0 TPs and 18 FPs — [V] (p. 8, p. 15).
4. LLM baselines: single-function CoT finds 1 true bug, multiple-function CoT 10; LLMDFA needs on average 165.23× (path scan) to 1,737.04× (source scan) the prompts and 123.18×–1,193.63× the input tokens of RepoAudit — [V] (p. 8, p. 14).
5. Ablations: without abstraction TPs −47.50%, FPs +181.82% (precision 40.38%); without validators FPs +245.45% (31 FPs); without caching ~3.5× prompts, 3.48× cost, 3.75× time, icu exceeds 72 h — [V] (p. 8, p. 15, p. 16).
6. Other models: DeepSeek R1 46 TPs, precision 88.46%, $0.57 but 4,571.72 s per project; Claude 3.7 Sonnet 46 TPs, 86.79%, $1.59; o3-mini 42 TPs, 82.35%, $0.30, ~625 s — [V] (p. 8, p. 16, p. 17).
7. Temperature 0–1.0: precision 72.92–78.43%, recall of known bugs 85.71–100% — [V] (p. 17, Table 11).
8. Field study on 9 projects: 185 new bugs reported at 85.71% precision; 95 confirmed and 79 fixed (174 total); libuv (86 NPD) and memcached (67 MLK) dominate — [V] (p. 8, p. 9, Table 4).
9. 19 of 25 CWE Top-25 (2024) weaknesses need global path-sensitive reasoning — [A] (p. 3).

# Benchmarking LLMs and LLM-based Agents in Practical Vulnerability Detection for Code Repositories

- Authors: Yildiz, Alperen; Teo, Sin G; Lou, Yiling; Feng, Yebo; Wang, Chong; Divakaran, Dinil Mon
- Year / venue: 2025, Proceedings of the 63rd Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (Volume 1: Long Papers) (published)
- DOI: 10.18653/v1/2025.acl-long.1490 · URL: https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.acl-long.1490
- Source text: `fulltext.txt` (page refs = PDF page index; printed pages 30848–30865). Identity confirmed: title and authors on p. 1 match metadata.

Labels: **[V]** verified in the paper's own data/tables/results · **[A]** author claim · **[I]** our interpretation.

## 1. Research objective
Provide a practical benchmark for just-in-time (JIT, i.e. commit-triggered) repository-level vulnerability detection with interprocedural context and pairwise (vulnerable vs patched) evaluation, and use it to compare LLMs with LLM-based ReAct agents [A] (p. 1, p. 2).

## 2. Research questions / hypotheses
- No numbered RQs. Four comparisons: detection method (p. 7), prompting strategy (p. 8), foundation model (p. 8), pairwise error analysis (p. 8) [V].
- Three stated requirements for practical benchmarks: interprocedural context, scalability, pairwise comparison [V] (p. 3, p. 4).

## 3. Methodology
- Research design: benchmark construction plus comparative evaluation [V] (p. 4, p. 5).
- Approach / model / intervention: (1) Plain LLM — target function only; (2) Dep-Aug LLM — adds Top-5 lexically similar callers/callees (Jaccard), reproducing VulEval; (3) ReAct agent with tools get_callers, get_callees, get_definition (CFlow + CTags, LangChain) [V] (p. 5, p. 6). Each in four variants: vanilla, CoT ("Solve this problem step by step..."), few-shot (10 hand-made vulnerable/benign pairs from CWE Top-25 web pages), CoT+FS [V] (p. 5, p. 6).
- Baselines or comparison conditions: the three LLM settings against each other; no SAST tools, no fine-tuned deep-learning detectors [V] (p. 5). A random-guessing reference (22.70%) is cited from Ding et al. (p. 7).

## 4. Dataset and sample
- Data source(s): PrimeVul (CVE fixed in a single commit modifying a single function); vul-intro commit approximated by tracing the target function's history back to its last modification into the vulnerable version [V] (p. 4, p. 5).
- Sample size (N) and unit: 879 CVEs, 91 CWEs (max 10 CVEs per CWE), 1,758 samples (879 vul, 879 ben) [V] (p. 4, p. 5). Repositories: mean 2,955.9 files, 91% C / 9% C++, mean 988.8 KLoC (median 297.6, SD 1,991.2); target function mean 696.4 LOC [V] (p. 5).
- Sampling / selection method: random selection of 879 PrimeVul CVEs ensuring CWE coverage [V] (p. 4).
- Data availability (public? link?): "We release all code and data at this repository" (p. 2); the URL is a hyperlink not present in the extracted text.

## 5. Experimental setup
- Procedure, settings, hyper-parameters, tools: GPT-4o-mini and GPT-4o at temperature 0; Transformers v4.52.0, LangChain v0.3.14 [V] (p. 6). Llama-3.1-8B and DeepSeek-Coder-V2-16B in Appendix B [V] (p. 8, p. 15). Number of runs: not reported (one implied by temperature 0). Number of pairs evaluated per setting: not reported (see Analyst notes). Token use, cost and runtime: not reported.
- Code availability: yes, per p. 2.

## 6. Evaluation metrics
- Metric — what it measures — why chosen: F1 (common metric) and pairwise accuracy pAcc = share of pairs where both versions are labelled correctly, inspired by PrimeVul, to test whether methods capture the vulnerability rather than surface patterns [V] (p. 6). Error types: pairwise vulnerable, pairwise benign, pairwise reversed [V] (p. 8).
- Statistical testing (tests used, significance, effect sizes, confidence intervals): none reported.

## 7. Key findings
1. GPT-4o-mini F1/pAcc — Plain: 56.00/3.36 (vanilla), 65.10/3.36 (CoT), 48.74/7.56 (FS), 64.65/11.76 (CoT+FS); Dep-Aug: 52.68/2.05, 66.05/4.86, 48.23/7.27, 65.01/4.68; ReAct: 56.63/12.61, 56.93/16.81, 56.81/20.17, 51.06/14.29 — [V] (p. 6, Table 2).

3. ReAct agents have higher pAcc than other methods for every prompting strategy (gains 0.1 to 16.61 points); Plain/Dep-Aug have higher F1 in most settings (4.15–13.95 points) — [V] (p. 7). Average ReAct pAcc gain over Dep-Aug: 9.46 (4o-mini) and 8.42 (4o) — [V] (p. 7).

## 1. Research objective
Disentangle the separate effects of reasoning ("thinking") modes, agentic designs and explanation quality on LLM vulnerability detection, which prior studies examined separately or under heterogeneous settings [A] (abstract).

## 2. Research questions / hypotheses
Three phases per the abstract [A] (abstract): (1) instruct vs thinking variants of four open models, zero- and few-shot, no agents; (2) single-, dual- and multi-agent designs with call-matched single-agent baselines, alert-burden metrics and cost analysis; (3) explanation quality via blinded human and calibrated LLM evaluation over correct and incorrect predictions, controlling for response length and model family. ## 7. Key findings
1. Thinking improves non-agent F1 in 7 of 8 settings, mainly through recall — [A] (abstract).
2. The dual-agent design achieves the highest F1 and recall but flags 88.2% of functions as vulnerable — [A] (abstract).
3. Multi-agent performs better on pairwise discrimination, but call-matched self-revision achieves the best P-C overall — [A] (abstract).
4. Thinking uses 2.7–4.4× more energy, and its F1 benefit declines with greater orchestration — [A] (abstract).
5. Explanation gains are model-specific, partly length-mediated, and do not distinguish correct from incorrect predictions — [A] (abstract).
