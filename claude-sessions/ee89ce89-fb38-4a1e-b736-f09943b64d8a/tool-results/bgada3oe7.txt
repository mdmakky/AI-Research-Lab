## Positioning of our review

**Question.** Does a review or SLR already compare LLM-based, SAST and DL vulnerability detection through an evaluation-setup / benchmark-realism lens, i.e. arguing that benchmark design explains contradictory results?

**Verdict.** We found no review that does both of the following (searches on 2026-11-12: Q1–Q5, Q21, Q22, W1, W2):
1. synthesises head-to-head evidence across all three families (LLM, SAST, DL) **plus** LLM+SAST hybrids;
2. explains specific contradictions between primary studies through their evaluation setup, with a cross-paper comparability audit.

Several reviews cover parts of this, so the claim of novelty must be narrow and precise. **Confidence: medium.** The closest competing review (qin2026review, the CAI 2026 review) has now been read in full and confirms the difference (see below). The other near-miss reviews were read at abstract level only, and the field publishes very fast.

**The four reviews in our corpus.**

Deep analysis of datasets, custom splits and leakage [@kaniewski2026systematic, p. 35; @kaniewski2026systematic, p. 38]. Excludes studies that only post-process tool output [@kaniewski2026systematic, p. 8]. | It does not cover the LLM-filters-SAST family (C2) and does not synthesise LLM-vs-SAST results. **How our review should position itself** [I]:
1. **Cross-family scope.** It covers all three families plus both hybrid types (C1, C2), which none of the reviews above combines.
2. **Contradiction-driven synthesis.** Six named contradictions are each traced to differences in metric, data, unit, labeller or scoring (methodology comparison §4). Examples:
   - fine-tuned models vs GPT-4 reverse between Big-Vul F1 and PrimeVul pairwise scoring [@fu2023chatgpt, p. 4; @ding2025vulnerability, p. 9];
   - class balance flips rankings [@shu2026evaluating, p. 40];
   - scoring leniency favours LLMs [@gnieciak2025large, p. 6].
3. **Explicit comparability audit.** We state which cross-paper numbers may and may not be compared (§3). For example, CodeQL on OWASP replicates across two groups [@khare2025understanding, p. 9; @xiong2026sifting, p. 11], while Big-Vul F1 is not comparable.
4. **Page-level verification and baseline checks.** We check results against trivial baselines (W1), which reviews of headline numbers do not do.

**Risk.** With 24 papers (20 primary studies), the review must be framed as a *critical comparative review*, not a comprehensive SLR. Reviewers will likely point to qin2026review (the CAI 2026 review) and kaniewski2026systematic for the "evaluation pitfalls" message. The distinct contribution is the cross-family, contradiction-explaining angle, and the G1 re-analysis below would make it concrete.

## G1: Published results are not re-scored against trivial baselines or realistic prevalence; no shared evaluation protocol exists for comparing LLMs, SAST and DL
- Gap type: methodological
- Evidence that the gap exists:
  - **Results sit at the flag-everything level.** On 50/50 data, "flag everything" gives F1 0.667. Studies with balanced data report best values at or below that level:
    - shu: the dummy classifier scores 0.6693 against a best of 0.7075 [@shu2026evaluating, p. 11; @shu2026evaluating, p. 17] [V];
    - tan: best F1 0.665–0.666 [@tan2026think, p. 7];
    - yildiz: GPT F1 48–66 [@yildiz2025benchmarking, p. 6];
    - khare: always-vulnerable rows appear among the best F1 values [@khare2025understanding, p. 16].
  - **The same pattern holds at other prevalences, in three more recent studies** (second pass). Two of them report trivial baselines themselves; for the third the chance level is derivable:
    - deininger (35.4% vulnerable sample): LLM F1 0.405–0.615 against always-vulnerable F1 0.523 [@deininger2026open, p. 7] [V]. At the natural 1:44 prevalence every LLM's precision is 2.3–3.8% against 2.21% for always-flag, with 25.6–41.9 false positives per true positive [@deininger2026open, p. 8] [V] (2.8–4.6% in the budget-matched second epoch [@deininger2026open, p. 17]).
    - tashenova (PHP): a one-feature token-count baseline matches or beats the TF-IDF random forest in 4 of 6 settings and is within 0.001 macro-F1 of the Bi-LSTM [@tashenova2026real, p. 8] [V]. The authors ask that future detectors "be required to clear such trivial baselines" [@tashenova2026real, p. 13] [A].
    - lu (VentiVul, post-May-2025 CVEs, prevalence 263/13,326 = 1.97% [@lu2025lab, p. 4]): DL F1 is 3.47–8.65 [@lu2025lab, p. 13] [V]. The paper reports no trivial baseline; by our calculation the all-positive F1 is 3.87%, and 7 of the 12 LLM conservative F1 values fall below it [@lu2025lab, p. 14] [I].
  - **Class balance flips rankings.**
    - shu: fine-tuned GPT-4o wins on balanced data, but PLMs win on the 17%-vulnerable test [@shu2026evaluating, p. 17; @shu2026evaluating, p. 40] [V].
    - yildiz: F1 falls from about 49–65 to 5.41–12.21 on non-paired data [@yildiz2025benchmarking, p. 7] [V].
  - **Scoring is unequal between families.**
    - gnieciak applies a lenient location rule only to LLMs [@gnieciak2025large, p. 6] [V].
    - The CASTLE prompt reveals the scoring scheme to the LLM [@dubniczky2025castle, p. 9] [V].
    - Statement-level scoring halves F1 [@ahmed2026secvuleval, p. 8] [V].
  - **Headline LLM-vs-SAST numbers use five incompatible metrics**: macro F1, CASTLE score, accuracy, detection ratio, recall/SFDR [@gnieciak2025large, p. 10; @dubniczky2025castle, p. 10; @khare2025understanding, p. 9; @zhou2024comparison, p. 5; @li2026llm, p. 11] [I].
  - **Spurious baselines and random references:**
    - a word-count classifier reaches F1 0.86 on BigVul [@risse2025top, p. 11] [V];
    - GPT-4 pairwise correctness is below the 22.70% random reference [@ding2025vulnerability, p. 9] [V].
  - **Statistics are rare.** Among the first 16 primary studies, only tan2026think applies significance tests to its main comparison [@tan2026think, p. 6] [I, our count]. Of the four second-pass papers, deininger2026open uses a paired bootstrap with Holm correction [@deininger2026open, p. 6] [V].
  - **The decision rule and the prompt can flip conclusions.** At the default argmax threshold the fine-tuned PrimeVul detector predicts all-safe despite ROC-AUC 0.845; the authors nearly reported this as "trained detectors are no better than LLMs" [@deininger2026open, p. 11] [V]. Prompt paraphrases change the winning LLM [@deininger2026open, p. 20] [V].
  - **Authors call for guidelines.** yildiz2025benchmarking calls for evaluation guidelines for JIT benchmarking [@yildiz2025benchmarking, p. 2] [A]. ding2025vulnerability proposes VD-S because accuracy and F1 mislead [@ding2025vulnerability, p. 2] [A]. lu2025lab asks for cross-dataset and temporally separated testing plus patch-pair metrics [@lu2025lab, p. 16] [A].
- Novelty check (2026-11-12; first pass Q4, Q5, Q9, Q10 on OpenAlex/arXiv from 2024/2025 and W4; second pass Q35 on OpenAlex/Crossref from 2025). Closest work:
  - **In our corpus, within-study baselines only.** shu2026evaluating, deininger2026open and tashenova2026real report trivial baselines, and lu2025lab's chance level is derivable (see evidence above). PrimeVul's VD-S is also in the corpus. Each applies the baseline only to its own results; none re-scores other studies [I].
  - **qin2026review** (in corpus) recommends better reporting [@qin2026review, p. 13] but recomputes no published results; its only quantitative re-report repeats ding2025vulnerability's table [@qin2026review, p. 12; @ding2025vulnerability, p. 7] [I].
  - *Pitfalls in Machine Learning for Computer Security* (CACM 2024, doi:10.1145/3643456) and *Pitfalls in LMs for Code Intelligence* (TOSEM 2025, doi:10.1145/3748647): generic pitfall catalogues with no re-scoring of vulnerability-detection results.
  - *How much model does warning triage need?* (American Impact Review 2026, doi:10.66308/air.e80134906): re-scores models against a simple reference with MCC and bootstrap CIs, but for actionable-warning triage, not vulnerability detection, and in an unknown-tier venue.
  - *On-premise open-weight LLMs for interpretable web vulnerability detection* (TSARKA Science 2026, doi:10.66571/tsarka-3134-6057-11; abstract only, from Q32): notes that several configurations "approach the score of a classifier labelling every input as vulnerable" and proposes reporting specificity. Again within one study (120 snippets).
  - Q35 returned no relevant hit.

  We found no study that recomputes published LLM, SAST and DL vulnerability-detection results *across studies* against trivial baselines and realistic prevalence (searches on 2026-11-12).
- **Second-pass verdict: unchanged (still open).** Trivial baselines are spreading as a *within-study* practice. This makes the gap's premise stronger: four further studies (deininger, tashenova, lu, and the abstract-only TSARKA study) show results at or near trivial levels. It does not fill the gap, because none of them re-scores other studies' published numbers on a common scale [I]. The contribution should now be framed as a *systematic cross-study* re-scoring, and it must credit these within-study baselines rather than claim to be the first to use them.
- Confidence: **medium–high**. The evidence now comes from ≥9 independent primary studies. The only risk is an unpublished or non-indexed meta-analysis.
- Potential contribution: a "re-scored results table" that makes the review's central claim concrete. Many reported LLM advantages may vanish or reverse once compared with flag-all baselines and evaluated at realistic prevalence. It also yields a minimal reporting checklist (confusion matrix, prevalence, trivial baselines, unit and location rule, runs/variance).
- Suggested study (secondary analysis of published numbers; no model runs):
  - **Data:** results tables of the 20 primary studies (plus any added papers). Extract every (study, tool, dataset, configuration) row that reports enough to rebuild a confusion matrix: TP/FP/FN/TN, or P, R and prevalence.
  - **Sample:** all extractable rows. Rows without prevalence or FPR are listed separately, not dropped silently.
  - **Method:** for each row compute:
    - MCC;
    - Youden's J = recall − FPR;
    - ΔF1 = F1 − F1_flag-all, where F1_flag-all = 2π/(1+π) and π is prevalence;
    - precision at deployment prevalence (π = 1%, 5%, 17%) via P = R·π / (R·π + FPR·(1−π)).
  - **Baselines:** always-vulnerable; random (P-C reference 22.70% for ding-style pairs); published dummy, word-count or token-count baselines where available. Use a study's own published baseline where it exists (shu, deininger, tashenova) and check that our formula reproduces it; this is a built-in validity check of the re-scoring.
  - **Analysis:** within each study, rank tools before and after re-scoring and count rank reversals (Kendall τ). Do not pool across datasets marked "not comparable" in methodology comparison §3. Report how many rows fail to beat flag-all.
  - **Threats:** many rows lack TN/FPR (selection bias); rounding in published tables; lenient TP definitions cannot be undone after the fact; transcription errors (mitigate by double extraction and spot-checking against PDFs).
- Feasibility for a beginner: **easy**. Needs only a spreadsheet or about 50 lines of Python, no GPU and no API cost, and takes about 2–4 weeks. It can be included directly in the comparative review as an added-value table.

## G2: No same-benchmark, leakage-controlled comparison of LLMs vs semantic SAST vs fine-tuned DL on real, post-cutoff code with file or repository context
- Gap type: evidence / methodological
- **Second-pass verdict: narrowed.** deininger2026open fills the function-level, pre-cutoff, C/C++ version of this gap with lightweight SAST. What remains open: (a) post-cutoff targets, (b) file or repository context, (c) a semantic, build-based SAST arm such as CodeQL, and (d) a peer-reviewed non-C/C++ version (a function-level Python/Java/JS three-family preprint exists, abstract only; see novelty check).
- Evidence that the gap exists:
  - **Two studies in our corpus run all three families; each covers only part of the needed setting.**
    - khare2025understanding evaluates LLMs, SAST and DL, but CodeQL runs on synthetic data only [@khare2025understanding, p. 7] [V].
    - deininger2026open runs 8 zero-shot LLMs, Flawfinder 2.0.20, Cppcheck 2.21, Semgrep 1.175 and two CodeBERT detectors on the same 1549 PrimeVul C/C++ functions [@deininger2026open, p. 6; @deininger2026open, p. 10] [V]. Its limits are the ones that define what is left of G2:
      - CodeQL could not be run because the functions are unbuildable snippets [@deininger2026open, p. 10] [V];
      - all CVEs date from 2008–2022 and predate the models' training cutoffs, and recall is 7–15 points higher on pre-2020 CVEs [@deininger2026open, p. 24] [V];
      - function level, one dataset, C/C++ only [@deininger2026open, p. 25] [A];
      - the fine-tuned detector is in-distribution while the LLMs are zero-shot [@deininger2026open, p. 11] [A].
    - Its result shows why the gap still matters: the ranking depends on the metric. On balanced accuracy fine-tuned CodeBERT leads (0.765 vs ≤0.711 for LLMs); on precision at 1:44 prevalence Semgrep (26.0%, CI 5.7–66.9%) and strict Flawfinder (13.4%) lead, and every LLM is at 2.3–3.8% in the first epoch (2.8–4.6% budget-matched) [@deininger2026open, p. 8; @deininger2026open, p. 10; @deininger2026open, p. 17] [V].
  - **zhou2024comparison has no classic DL detector.** It compares fine-tuned and prompted LLMs with SAST [@zhou2024comparison, p. 4] [V].
  - **Temporal out-of-distribution evidence exists, but without SAST.** lu2025lab tests DL and four open LLMs on VentiVul, built from Linux and Chromium CVEs disclosed from 1 May 2025 [@lu2025lab, p. 4] [V]. LineVul falls from F1 91–94.5 in distribution [@lu2025lab, p. 12] to 4.39–6.87 on VentiVul, and all 8 DL configurations score 3.47–8.65 [@lu2025lab, p. 13] [V]. No static-analysis tool is evaluated [I]. The authors cannot fully exclude LLM contamination because pre-training cutoffs are unknown [@lu2025lab, p. 17] [A].
  - **PHP evidence exists, but without LLMs or a standard SAST tool.** tashenova2026real compares an in-house taint analyser with ML and LSTM detectors under plugin-grouped, de-duplicated folds [@tashenova2026real, p. 5] [V]; no LLM or transformer was run [@tashenova2026real, p. 13] [V].
  - **Studies that call for this comparison:**
    - chakraborty2024revisiting: evaluate static and dynamic analysis tools on Real-Vul [@chakraborty2024revisiting, p. 13] [A];
    - ding2025vulnerability: hybrid LM + program-analysis systems [@ding2025vulnerability, p. 10] [A].
  - **Results reverse across setups.**
    - LLM vs SAST rankings reverse between small programs, synthetic suites and repositories [@gnieciak2025large, p. 10; @khare2025understanding, p. 9; @li2026llm, p. 7] [V].
    - LLM vs DL rankings reverse with dataset and class balance [@fu2023chatgpt, p. 4; @ding2025vulnerability, p. 9; @shu2026evaluating, p. 40] [V].
  - **Contamination and leakage are rarely controlled.**
    - Exact-copy leakage reaches up to 18.9% [@ding2025vulnerability, p. 4] [V].
    - Benchmarks are probably in pre-training data [@kaniewski2026systematic, p. 30] [A], and post-cutoff datasets are recommended [@kaniewski2026systematic, p. 38] [A].
    - Only xiong2026sifting, li2026llm and lu2025lab test on post-cutoff or temporally separated code [@xiong2026sifting, p. 15; @li2026llm, p. 5; @lu2025lab, p. 4] [V]; none of the three runs all three families [I].
  - **Survey future work asks only for verified benchmarks.** The surveys' future directions call for verified, contamination-free, repository-level benchmarks [@zhou2025large, p. 22; @sheng2025llms, p. 25] [A], but not for a controlled three-family comparison [I]. deininger2026open plans a post-cutoff restriction [@deininger2026open, p. 24] [A].
- Novelty check (2026-11-12; first pass Q6, Q7, Q8 on OpenAlex/arXiv/Crossref from 2024/2025, contamination angle Q19, Q20, W3, W6; second pass Q27, Q28, Q29 on OpenAlex/Crossref from 2025, arXiv abstract page A2, W10).
  - **Now in our corpus (analysed):** deininger2026open (three families, function level, pre-cutoff, lightweight SAST), lu2025lab (post-cutoff, no SAST), tashenova2026real (PHP, no LLM). See evidence above.
  - **Dahiya et al. 2026**, *Are Frontier LLMs Ready for Cybersecurity? Evidence for Vertical Foundation Models from Dual-Mode Vulnerability Benchmarks* (arXiv 2605.23243, preprint, v5 June 2026; **abstract only**, A2). Six frontier and two domain-specialised models on (i) white-box *function-level* detection in C/Java/Python built on VulnLLM-R and (ii) black-box testing of five web applications with 118 known vulnerabilities. From the abstract: no SAST baseline (the only tools named are Playwright and Burp Suite MCP servers for black-box testing), no fine-tuned DL detector, no post-cutoff or leakage control mentioned. Its "application-level" track is black-box dynamic testing, not source-code analysis. **Does not fill G2.**
  - **Zhang et al. 2025**, *Benchmarking Large Language Models for Multi-Language Software Vulnerability Detection* (arXiv 2503.01449, preprint; **abstract only**, Q27/W9). Five open LLMs (prompting, instruction tuning, fine-tuning) against five fine-tuned small language models and two open-source SAST tools, on function-level data in Python (8,260 vulnerable functions), Java (7,505) and JavaScript (28,983). Secondary summaries (W9) report that the SAST tools were less effective than both model families. This is a second same-benchmark three-family comparison and the only one outside C/C++ we found. From the abstract: function level, no temporal or post-cutoff separation mentioned, SAST tools not named. It was already returned by first-pass searches (Q17) but was missed in the first version of this report. It narrows the non-C/C++ part of G2 at function level; it does not address post-cutoff code, context or semantic SAST.
  - *Leveraging Large Language Models for advanced static code analysis: Assessing the feasibility of AI superseding traditional vulnerability detection methods* (Information and Software Technology 2026, doi:10.1016/j.infsof.2026.108213; Q32): **title only**, no abstract retrieved (W12). Its scope cannot be judged; it should be screened.
  - *RealVuln* (arXiv 2604.13764): SAST vs LLMs vs specialised scanners on 26 intentionally vulnerable Python repositories. **No DL; not real-world CVEs; the authors' company product is evaluated.**
  - *A Comparative Evaluation of LLMs for Real-World Vulnerability Detection: Big-Vul and CVEfixes* (IJANSER 2026, OpenAlex W7218261049): LLMs vs static baselines on balanced Big-Vul/CVEfixes. **No DL; noisy, non-post-cutoff data.**
  - *VulEval* (arXiv 2404.15596, reportedly TSE 2026): repository-level evaluation of DL and LLM methods. From its abstract, no SAST arm.
  - Contamination studies of LLM-only detection, which leave the cross-family comparison open:
    - CWE-Trace (arXiv 2606.20502);
    - BenchGuard (LNCS 2026, doi:10.1007/978-3-032-33260-8_22);
    - the ASE 2025 industry-showcase paper "Should We Evaluate LLM Based Security Analysis Approaches on Open Source Systems" (no identifier returned).

  We found no study that runs semantic SAST, fine-tuned DL and LLMs on the same real-world, de-duplicated, post-cutoff targets with file or repository context and a shared unit and metric (searches on 2026-11-12). Function-level, pre-cutoff three-family comparisons do exist (deininger2026open for C/C++; arXiv 2503.01449 for Python/Java/JS, abstract only).
- Confidence: **medium** (narrowed). The remaining gap is more specific, so it is easier to defend, but near misses now lack only one or two conditions (post-cutoff, context, semantic SAST). Re-check before submission.
- Potential contribution: the first like-for-like answer to RQ1 on realistic, post-cutoff code, and an estimate of how much of the LLM advantage is due to contamination (pre- vs post-cutoff subsets; deininger2026open already finds a recall gradient by CVE year [@deininger2026open, p. 24]). It would also test whether deininger's metric-dependent ranking (DL best on balanced accuracy, SAST best on precision) survives with context and semantic SAST. This also absorbs candidate gap 7 (contamination).
- Suggested study:
  - **Data:** Python (build-free for SAST) or Java CVEs. Collect vulnerability-fixing commits from GitHub Security Advisories/OSV (CVEfixes or MoreFixes pipeline). Split into a *post-cutoff* set (disclosed after the newest evaluated model's training cutoff) and a *pre-cutoff* set of the same size.
  - **Sample:** about 100 post-cutoff and 100 pre-cutoff CVEs. Use the vulnerable function before the fix plus its patched version (pairs), and add non-changed functions from the same files to reach a realistic ratio (e.g. 1:20). Manually verify a random 50 positives (two raters, Cohen's κ), as in risse2025top.
  - **Arms:**
    - SAST: Semgrep (security rulesets) and CodeQL `security-extended`, default and tuned query suites.
    - DL: UniXcoder or CodeBERT fine-tuned on pre-cutoff data with project-disjoint splits.
    - LLM: 2–3 open 7–14B models (local) and optionally one API model, zero-shot and chain-of-thought, 3 runs each.
  - **Unit mapping:** a SAST warning counts for the function that contains its line. Also report file-level results so that SAST is not penalised by the mapping.
  - **Metrics:** recall, precision and MCC at the natural ratio; pairwise correctness on pairs; flag rate; cost (USD, GPU-hours, wall time).
  - **Baselines:** flag-all, random, and a function-length classifier (competitive in tashenova2026real [@tashenova2026real, p. 8]).
  - **Analysis:** McNemar tests between arms on identical functions; project-level bootstrap CIs; pre- vs post-cutoff difference per arm as a contamination estimate.
  - **Threats:**
    - noisy fix-commit labels;
    - function mapping disadvantages SAST;
    - small fine-tuning data for Python;
    - prompt sensitivity;
    - residual contamination (advisories published before the CVE date);
    - limited generalisation beyond one language.
- Feasibility for a beginner: **moderate**. All tools are free, and a 7B model runs on Colab or a laptop GPU. The hard parts are dataset construction and mapping warnings to functions. Expect 6–10 weeks. A low-cost variant reuses VentiVul from lu2025lab (post-cutoff, data public [@lu2025lab, p. 2]) and adds a SAST arm, an LLM arm with file context, and an all-positive baseline. Run Flawfinder, Cppcheck and Semgrep on the whole source files, as deininger2026open did on functions; a CodeQL arm requires building the Linux or Chromium versions, which deininger2026open could not do for snippets [@deininger2026open, p. 10] and which is hard for a beginner.

## G3: True-positive loss in LLM filtering of SAST alarms is not controlled per CWE on real security alerts
- Gap type: practical / methodological
- Evidence that the gap exists:
  - **xiong2026sifting.** The best agent configuration cuts OWASP FPR from 98.3% to 6.3% [@xiong2026sifting, p. 12] but drops 22.25% of true vulnerabilities [@xiong2026sifting, p. 18] [V].
    - The miss rate is low for injection CWEs (CWE-78 2.38%, CWE-89 1.10%, CWE-79 0.41%) [V].
    - It is 77.17% for weak cryptography and 84.50% for weak hashing [@xiong2026sifting, p. 18] [V].
    - The authors plan "CWE-aware, cost-adaptive agent strategies and human-in-the-loop" auditing [@xiong2026sifting, p. 20] [A].
  - **du2026reducing.** Removes 94–98% of false alarms with real-bug recall of 0.75–0.88 [@du2026reducing, p. 8] [V]. This covers non-security bug types and the authors' own method.
  - **Recommended but not evaluated.** kaniewski2026systematic recommends SAST → LLM false-positive filtering pipelines [@kaniewski2026systematic, p. 42] [A] but its corpus excludes them [@kaniewski2026systematic, p. 8].
  - **Real-world samples are small.** xiong uses 50 alerts per set [@xiong2026sifting, p. 13] [V].
- Novelty check (2026-11-12; Q13, Q14, Q24 on OpenAlex/arXiv/Crossref from 2024/2025, plus Q25; W5). Closest hits, all not analysed:
  - *ZeroFalse* (arXiv 2510.02534): CWE-specific context, high F1 on OWASP/OpenVuln, claims to preserve coverage.
  - *Using LLMs to Adjudicate Static-Analysis Alerts with Error Reduction Techniques* (arXiv 2607.09979): consistency checks reach ≥98% recall on Juliet, FormAI and SV-COMP, which are synthetic suites.
  - *QASecClaw* (arXiv 2605.01885): multi-agent FP reduction on OWASP.
  - *Case-Level Verification in Scanner-LLM Cascades* (arXiv 2610.08406): formalises the FP-reduction vs TPR trade-off, but for DAST.
  - *Trusting the Tool* (arXiv 2610.32876): false analyser assurances lower detection of real vulnerabilities by up to 19 points.

  These show that TP loss is being measured, and can be reduced on synthetic suites. We found no study that tests a CWE-aware selective filtering policy, i.e. one that filters only CWE classes with demonstrated low miss rates, with held-out evaluation and full FP-reduction vs TP-retention curves on real-world security alerts (searches on 2026-11-12).
- Confidence: **low–medium**. This is an active area with many 2025–26 preprints. The gap is narrow and could be filled quickly.
- **Second-pass verdict: unchanged.** None of the four new papers studies LLM filtering of SAST alarms, and the second-pass cost/triage queries (Q30, Q31) surfaced only items already listed above (arXiv 2607.09979) or non-security triage work. Not re-searched separately.
- Potential contribution: an operating-point view of alarm filtering, i.e. what share of real vulnerabilities is lost per CWE for a given FP reduction. Also a simple routing policy that practitioners can adopt.
- Suggested study:
  - **Data:**
    - OWASP Benchmark v1.2 (Java, 2,740 labelled cases, official scorer) for development.
    - A real-world set of about 100 SAST alerts from post-cutoff open-source Java projects, labelled by two raters (κ), for testing.
  - **Method:** run Semgrep and CodeQL; ask 2 open LLMs (7–8B) for a TP/FP verdict with a confidence score, 3 runs.
  - **Policies:**
    - no filter;
    - filter-all (xiong's vanilla);
    - CWE-aware filter that applies the LLM only to CWEs whose miss rate was below 5% on a development half of OWASP, evaluated on the other half and on the real set.
  - **Metrics:** FP-reduction rate; TP retention; residual FPR; expected triage cost = c_FP·FP + c_FN·FN for cost ratios 1:10 to 1:100.
  - **Analysis:** curves across confidence thresholds; per-CWE bootstrap CIs.
  - **Threats:** OWASP is synthetic and probably in pre-training data; per-CWE cells are small; rater bias.
- Feasibility for a beginner: **easy–moderate**. Ground truth and the scorer are public, Java SAST on OWASP builds easily, and 7B models run locally. Expect 4–6 weeks. The main effort is labelling the real-world alerts.

## G4: Hybrid LLM+SAST detectors lack independent replication that separates target, metric and labeller effects
- Gap type: contradiction / evidence
- Evidence that the gap exists:
  - **RepoAudit: author evaluation vs independent evaluation.**
    - Authors: 78.43% precision and all 21 known bugs reproduced, one run per setting [@guo2025repoaudit, p. 8; @guo2025repoaudit, p. 17] [V].
    - Independent (li2026llm): 0 of 110 known C/C++ vulnerabilities and 92.77% SFDR [@li2026llm, p. 7; @li2026llm, p. 11] [V]; cost up to $1,247.66 per project [@li2026llm, p. 16] [V].
  - **IRIS: author evaluation vs independent evaluation.**
    - Authors: 55 vs 27 of 120 CVEs detected compared with CodeQL [@li2024iris, p. 8] [V].
    - Independent: 97.80% vs 77.00% SFDR on new Java projects [@li2026llm, p. 12] [V].
  - **The independent study labels with an LLM it also evaluates.** li2026llm labels warnings with Codex, which it also evaluates [@li2026llm, p. 11] [V], so its labeller is not independent [I].
  - **The gain depends on a strong LLM.** IRIS gains depend on GPT-4; smaller LLMs worsen FDR [@li2024iris, p. 10] [V].
  - **Neither paper settles the discrepancy** (methodology comparison §4, C2/C6) [I].
- Novelty check (2026-11-12; Q11, Q12, Q23 on arXiv/OpenAlex/Crossref from 2024/2025; W3). Closest hits:
  - li2026llm (in corpus) is itself the only independent re-evaluation.
  - *What Survives the Next Model?* (arXiv 2609.00468): re-runs ICSE 2026 technique papers against single prompts, across SE tasks generally.
  - *Trusting the Tool* (arXiv 2610.32876): studies LLM deference to analyser evidence, not tool replication.
  - Agent tools found (Revelio arXiv 2606.22263, AnyPoC arXiv 2604.11950) are new tools, not replications.

  We found no study that re-runs IRIS or RepoAudit on the authors' original benchmark and on new targets, under the same blinded human labelling, so that the discrepancy can be attributed (searches on 2026-11-12).
- Confidence: **medium**. Reproductions may exist in artifact-evaluation reports that are not indexed.
- **Second-pass verdict: unchanged.** None of the four new papers evaluates IRIS, RepoAudit or another hybrid LLM+SAST detector. Not re-searched separately.
- Potential contribution: tells practitioners whether published hybrid gains transfer to new code. It also tells researchers which evaluation choices (targets, metric, labeller, model version) drive the reported differences.
- Suggested study:
  - **Design:** 2 × 2 factorial — target set (original CWE-Bench-Java subset vs post-cutoff Java CVEs) × metric (the original "detected CVE" metric vs sampled false-discovery rate), on the **same** runs.
  - **Data:** 30 CWE-Bench-Java CVEs across 4 CWEs, plus 10–15 post-cutoff Java CVEs. IRIS code and data are public [@li2024iris, p. 2].
  - **Arms:** IRIS with the original LLM family where affordable, and with one open model; CodeQL alone as baseline.
  - **Labelling:** a random sample of ≥100 warnings labelled by two humans blinded to the tool (κ reported).
  - **Metrics:** detections, FDR/SFDR, cost (tokens, USD, time).
  - **Threats:** model deprecation (GPT-4 versions); CodeQL version drift; build failures; API cost.
- Feasibility for a beginner: **moderate–hard**. Building Java projects for CodeQL and running a hybrid pipeline is demanding. A reduced IRIS-only replication on 20–30 CVEs with an open model is feasible in about 8 weeks. RepoAudit (C/C++, high token cost) is not recommended for a beginner.

## G5: Real-CVE LLM-vs-standard-SAST evidence is missing for PHP and Go (JavaScript and Python only partly covered)
- Gap type: population/context
- **Second-pass verdict: narrowed.** LLM-only evidence on real WordPress-plugin (PHP) vulnerabilities now exists (Neef et al. 2026, abstract only), and a function-level three-family comparison exists for Python, Java and JavaScript (arXiv 2503.01449, abstract only). What remains open is a comparison of LLMs with *established* SAST tools on real CVEs in PHP and Go, preferably with file or plugin context.
- Evidence that the gap exists:
  - **The literature is concentrated on C/C++ and Java.**
    - Evidence is concentrated on C/C++ and Java [@kaniewski2026systematic, p. 28] [V].
    - 555 mappable CWEs appear in no analysed dataset [@kaniewski2026systematic, p. 34] [V].
    - Directions call for Python, Java, Rust and multilingual benchmarks [@kaniewski2026systematic, p. 40] [A].
  - **Authors list other languages as future work:**
    - RepoAudit: extension to Python and JavaScript [@guo2025repoaudit, p. 10] [A];
    - IRIS: other languages [@li2024iris, p. 10] [A];
    - du2026reducing: more languages [@du2026reducing, p. 10] [A];
    - xiong2026sifting: more languages and SAST tools [@xiong2026sifting, p. 20] [A];
    - li2026llm: broader languages and CWE classes [@li2026llm, p. 18] [A];
    - deininger2026open: other languages [@deininger2026open, p. 25] [A];
    - lu2025lab: VentiVul is C/C++ only and may not generalise to other languages [@lu2025lab, p. 17] [A].
  - **The few multi-language studies are thin or lack SAST.**
    - The multi-language LLM study in our corpus (REEF, 7 languages) has no SAST arm [@shu2026evaluating, p. 7] [V].
    - zhou2024comparison covers Python with roughly 8–10 test CVEs per language [@zhou2024comparison, p. 6] [I].
  - **The only PHP study in our corpus has no LLM and no standard SAST tool.** tashenova2026real builds a real-CVE WordPress-plugin benchmark (1757 CVEs, 1281 plugins) [@tashenova2026real, p. 3] [V]. Its SAST arm is an unnamed, in-house, intra-procedural lexical taint analyser [@tashenova2026real, p. 5; @tashenova2026real, p. 13] [V] that detects only 1.7–1.8% of vulnerabilities [@tashenova2026real, p. 9] [V]. The authors attribute the low recall to "that implementation rather than taint analysis in general" [@tashenova2026real, p. 13] [A]. No LLM or transformer was run [@tashenova2026real, p. 13] [V]. Its "misses about 98%" result therefore says nothing about established PHP SAST tools or about LLMs [I]. It works at function level, not plugin or repository level [I].
- Novelty check (2026-11-12; first pass Q17, Q18, Q26 on OpenAlex/arXiv from 2024/2025 and W8; second pass Q32 on OpenAlex/Crossref, Q33 on OpenAlex, Q34 on arXiv, all from 2025, arXiv abstract page A1, W9). Closest hits:
  - **Neef et al. 2026**, *Evaluating LLMs for Real-World Web Vulnerability Detection* (arXiv 2606.21397, v1 19 June 2026; to appear at the AI&CCPS Workshop at ARES 2026, LNCS, doi:10.1007/978-3-032-35576-8_3; **abstract only**, A1, Q32, Q34). Cited by deininger2026open as comparing open and frontier LLMs on WordPress-plugin web vulnerability detection [@deininger2026open, p. 3]. From the abstract:
    - six frontier and open-weight LLMs; real-world WordPress-plugin vulnerabilities (SQL injection, stored XSS, path traversal, RCE); five prompt designs; three iterations;
    - best detection rate 63% (Claude Opus 4.6), 48% for open-weight MiniMax M2.5, 35% for self-hosted Qwen 3.5; scoped prompts beat open-ended ones; no model was fully consistent across iterations; code and data are published;
    - **no SAST tool baseline** is named (the LLMs themselves perform "static analysis"); no fine-tuned DL detector; the number of plugins or CVEs and the input granularity (function, file or whole plugin) are not stated in the abstract; PHP is not named explicitly, but WordPress plugins are PHP code [I].
    - **Effect on G5:** it fills the "LLMs on real PHP CVEs" part, so G5 is **narrowed**. It does not fill the LLM-vs-SAST comparison. Whether it works at plugin (repository) level must be checked in the full text.
  - **Zhang et al. 2025** (arXiv 2503.01449, preprint; abstract only; see G2): function-level Python, Java and JavaScript, LLMs vs fine-tuned small models vs two open-source SAST tools. It covers JavaScript and Python at function level, not repository level.
  - *On-premise open-weight LLMs for interpretable web vulnerability detection* (TSARKA Science 2026, doi:10.66571/tsarka-3134-6057-11; abstract only, Q32): 120 annotated web-application snippets, five open models vs a rule-based static analyser and published cloud-model results. Snippet level, not real CVEs at repository level; venue tier unknown.
  - *Benchmarking Static Analysis for PHP Applications Security* (Entropy 2025, doi:10.3390/e27090926; abstract only, Q32): an evaluation framework for PHP SAST tools; no LLM.
  - *RealVuln* (arXiv 2604.13764): Python, but intentionally vulnerable repositories and a vendor author.
  - JavaScript:
    - *LLMs Cannot Reliably Detect Vulnerabilities in JavaScript* (arXiv 2512.01255): LLM-focused benchmark;
    - *Exploring Automated Vulnerability Identification in JavaScript Code Using LLMs* (arXiv 2609.13816): snippet-level, LLM vs SAST.
  - *Vulnerability Detection with Interprocedural Context in Multiple Languages* (arXiv 2604.08417): LLMs only, C/C++/Python.
  - *A Preliminary Study of LLMs for Multilingual Vulnerability Detection* (ISSTA companion 2025, doi:10.1145/3713081.3731746): PLMs vs LLMs, no SAST.
  - Go: Q33 returned no Go-specific LLM-vs-SAST study.

  We found no study comparing LLMs with an established SAST tool on real CVEs for PHP or Go, and only function-level, snippet-level or synthetic-repository LLM-vs-SAST evidence for JavaScript and Python (searches on 2026-11-12).
- Confidence: **low–medium** (narrowed). Python and JavaScript are being filled quickly, and the Neef et al. full text may turn out to include a tool baseline that its abstract does not mention. The gap is clearest for PHP-with-standard-SAST and for Go.
- Potential contribution: tests whether the "LLM higher recall, lower precision" pattern holds in web languages, where injection CWEs (which LLMs handle best [@xiong2026sifting, p. 18]) dominate.
- Suggested study:
  - **Data (PHP):** the expert-verified subset released with tashenova2026real, 125 vulnerable and 247 safe functions [@tashenova2026real, p. 11], plus the corresponding plugin versions (reconstructed from WordPress.org with the released loader [@tashenova2026real, p. 14]) for file-level runs. If Neef et al. 2026 released their plugin set (its abstract says code and data are published), reuse it so the LLM results can be compared with theirs. **Data (Go):** about 60 Go CVEs from the Go vulnerability database (OSV).
  - **Arms:** established SAST (Semgrep with PHP or Go rules; for PHP also a taint-mode tool such as Psalm or Progpilot; for Go, gosec), 2 open LLMs at function level and at file level with retrieved callers, the token-count baseline from tashenova2026real [@tashenova2026real, p. 8], and flag-all. Check CodeQL's supported-language list for the chosen language before planning a CodeQL arm.
  - **Metrics:** recall on known CVEs; precision on a manually labelled warning sample; cost.
  - **Analysis:** per-CWE recall with CIs.
  - **Threats:** label quality (tashenova2026real finds only 48.8% of cleaned positive labels genuine, single annotator [@tashenova2026real, p. 11]; using its expert-verified subset reduces this); small samples (125 positives give wide CIs; report Wilson intervals); tool coverage differences (some CWE classes are outside a tool's rules); old plugin versions may no longer be downloadable (about 54% of indexed CVEs were unrecoverable in tashenova2026real [@tashenova2026real, p. 3]); contamination (pre-2025 CVEs are likely in LLM pre-training data).
- Feasibility for a beginner: **moderate**. The tools are free and build-free for PHP and Go, and the PHP labels already exist. The main cost is manual labelling of warnings that fall outside the labelled subset.

## G6: No cost-per-confirmed-vulnerability comparison that includes human triage effort and a costed SAST and DL arm on the same targets
- Gap type: practical
- **Second-pass verdict: narrowed.** deininger2026open now reports compute cost (USD) and energy (kJ) per true positive at realistic prevalence for 8 LLMs. What remains open is (a) the human triage term, i.e. analyst time spent on the false positives that come with each true positive, and (b) a quantified cost per true positive for the SAST and fine-tuned DL arms on the same targets.
- Evidence that the gap exists:
  - **Most studies report no monetary cost.** 13 of our 20 primary studies report none [I, our count]. The seven that do are du, guo, li2026llm, shu, tan, xiong and deininger [@du2026reducing, p. 8; @guo2025repoaudit, p. 7; @li2026llm, p. 16; @shu2026evaluating, p. 42; @tan2026think, p. 10; @xiong2026sifting, p. 17; @deininger2026open, p. 7].
  - **Where measured, cost is decisive.**
    - Per project: from $2.54 in the RepoAudit paper to $172.77 on average and $1,247.66 at most in li2026llm [@guo2025repoaudit, p. 7; @li2026llm, p. 16] [V].
    - Thinking mode costs 2.7–4.4× the energy [@tan2026think, p. 10] [V]; in deininger2026open reasoning costs 4.5–12.1× more per task [@deininger2026open, p. 18] [V].
  - **Compute cost per true positive now exists for LLMs, but not for the other families and not for triage.**
    - deininger2026open reports USD 0.003 (Gemma-3-4B) to 0.212 (Claude-Sonnet-5) and 3.7 to 119.6 kJ per true positive at 1:44 prevalence [@deininger2026open, p. 8] [V].
    - Flawfinder is given as ≈0 cost per true positive [@deininger2026open, p. 8] [V]. No cost per true positive is quantified for CodeBERT ("negligible inference cost") or for Cppcheck and Semgrep [@deininger2026open, p. 8; @deininger2026open, p. 11] [V].
    - Each LLM true positive comes with 25.6–41.9 false positives [@deininger2026open, p. 8] [V]. The paper notes that no triage workflow absorbs that alert volume [@deininger2026open, p. 8] [A], but analyst time is not costed [I]. At such ratios analyst time would plausibly dominate compute cost of a few cents per true positive [I].
    - Caveat for anyone reusing Table 5: its kJ per true positive for Qwen3-Coder-30B (4.0) and Llama-3.3-70B (44.8) uses FLOP estimates, not the measured per-task energy (88 J and 738 J) [@deininger2026open, p. 7; @deininger2026open, p. 8; @deininger2026open, p. 14] [V]. Recomputed from the measured values these are about 8.8 and 34.9 kJ per true positive [I, from critical review D].
  - **Calls for audit-time measurement.** li2026llm calls for cost-aware designs [@li2026llm, p. 18] [A]. qin2026review recommends human-machine studies that record audit time per real vulnerability, and SAST-LLM systems evaluated by total audit cost [@qin2026review, p. 13] [A] (narrative review; not independent evidence).
- Novelty check (2026-11-12; first pass Q15, Q16, Q25 on OpenAlex/arXiv/Crossref from 2024/2025 and W7; second pass Q30 on OpenAlex/Crossref and Q31 on OpenAlex, both from 2025, and W11). Closest work:
  - **deininger2026open** (in corpus; see evidence above): compute cost and energy per true positive for LLMs; no triage term; SAST and DL arms not costed per true positive.
  - deininger2026open's related-work table rates Neef et al. (arXiv 2606.21397) and Dahiya et al. (arXiv 2605.23243) as giving partial treatment of cost [@deininger2026open, p. 3] [A]. Neither abstract mentions cost, a SAST arm or triage time (A1, A2; abstract only).
  - *Vulnerability Detection with Interprocedural Context in Multiple Languages* (arXiv 2604.08417): cost per LLM configuration only.
  - *Demystifying the Mythos or Disrupting Bugonomics?* (arXiv 2605.24632, preprint; abstract only, W11): argues qualitatively that validation and triage capacity, not bug finding, is the bottleneck; the abstract reports no measured triage time and no cost per vulnerability.
  - Grey literature (W11; not peer-reviewed): vendor benchmarks (Semgrep, June 2026) report dollars per true positive for their own products, and a practitioner write-up of an LLM-assisted audit reports API cost per confirmed finding *before* human validation. Both have conflicts of interest or no peer review, and neither includes analyst time.

  We found no peer-reviewed study reporting cost per confirmed vulnerability that includes human triage time, for LLM, SAST and DL arms on the same targets (searches on 2026-11-12).
- Confidence: **low–medium** (narrowed). What remains is specific (triage term plus costed non-LLM arms) and fits as a secondary outcome of G2.
- Potential contribution: a decision-oriented metric for practitioners: dollars and analyst minutes per real vulnerability found. It would extend deininger2026open's compute-only USD per true positive with the term that probably dominates it [I].
- Suggested study: measure as a secondary outcome of G2 (same targets). Record compute cost per arm (for SAST and DL: CPU/GPU time × a stated hourly rate, so that they are not left at "≈0"), then time two raters triaging a random 50 warnings per arm. Cost per confirmed TP = (compute + triage minutes × hourly rate) / confirmed TPs, reported with sensitivity bands for hourly rates and at realistic prevalence (e.g. 1:44 as in deininger2026open). Use measured, not FLOP-estimated, energy where possible. Threats: triage time depends on rater skill and on the order in which warnings are shown; API price changes and provider price spread (up to 14× for one model [@deininger2026open, p. 5]).
- Feasibility for a beginner: **moderate**. It is cheap if added to G2, but on its own it needs the same infrastructure.

# Research questions — LLM-based vs Traditional Static Analysis and Deep Learning Approaches for Source Code Vulnerability Detection: A Comparative Review

<!-- 3-5 questions. Each must be answerable from literature evidence. -->

## RQ1: How do LLM-based approaches compare with SAST tools and DL detectors in detection effectiveness (precision, recall, F1, false-positive rate) in head-to-head evaluations?
- Type: comparative
- Why it matters: the core practical question — which approach finds more real vulnerabilities with fewer false alarms.
- Linked objective: 1
- Status: answered (synthesis 2026-11-12; updated pass 2, 2026-11-12)
- Current answer (updated pass 2, 2026-11-12): No family wins across settings. Frontier LLMs beat SAST on small, self-contained code [@gnieciak2025large, p. 10; @dubniczky2025castle, p. 11], but CodeQL beats LLMs on synthetic function-level suites [@khare2025understanding, p. 9]. At project scale general coding agents reach far higher recall than SAST (59.55% vs 0–34.29% on C/C++), while most LLM detectors and SAST tools produce mostly false warnings [@li2026llm, p. 7; @li2026llm, p. 11]. On the same 1549 unpaired PrimeVul functions, seven of eight zero-shot LLMs beat lightweight SAST on balanced accuracy [@deininger2026open, p. 7; @deininger2026open, p. 10]. At the natural 1:44 prevalence, however, Flawfinder and Semgrep are more precise than every LLM, whose precision (2.3–4.6%) is barely above flag-all (2.21%) [@deininger2026open, p. 8; @deininger2026open, p. 17]. Versus fine-tuned DL, the ranking depends on dataset, class balance and decision threshold [@fu2023chatgpt, p. 4; @shu2026evaluating, p. 40; @deininger2026open, p. 11]. A fine-tuned CodeBERT beats all eight LLMs on those unpaired functions (balanced accuracy 0.765 vs ≤ 0.711) [@deininger2026open, p. 11; @deininger2026open, p. 17], and all families are near or below chance on PrimeVul pairs [@ding2025vulnerability, p. 9; @tan2026think, p. 6]. On 2025 CVEs at 1.97% prevalence, trained DL (F1 3.47–8.65) and four open LLMs ≤ 32B (0.79–5.36) both sit near the all-positive F1 of about 3.87% [I] [@lu2025lab, p. 13; @lu2025lab, p. 14]. For PHP, no LLM has been tested; a bespoke lexical taint analyser detects 1.7–1.8% of vulnerabilities [@tashenova2026real, p. 9]. **Strength: weak–moderate.** Most results come from single-run studies with incompatible metrics, and only one study compares all three families on identical real functions, with lightweight SAST. Most robust pattern [I]: LLMs have higher recall and lower precision, and at realistic prevalence their margin over trivial baselines is small.

## RQ2: How do evaluation setups (benchmarks, languages, CWE coverage, synthetic vs. real-world code, data-leakage control) differ across studies, and how do they explain disagreements in reported results?
- Type: methodological
- Why it matters: results that look contradictory may come from different benchmarks; this decides how much any number can be trusted.
- Linked objective: 2
- Status: answered (synthesis 2026-11-12; updated pass 2, 2026-11-12)
- Current answer (updated pass 2, 2026-11-12): Evaluation setup explains much of the disagreement. Sources of variation include:
  - label noise: 39–65% of positive labels correct [@risse2025top, p. 8], and 48.8% in a PHP benchmark [@tashenova2026real, p. 11];
  - leakage: exact-copy leakage up to 18.9% [@ding2025vulnerability, p. 4], and duplicate and contradictory-label groups even across plugin-grouped folds [@tashenova2026real, p. 4];
  - balanced vs realistic class ratios that flip rankings [@shu2026evaluating, p. 40; @yildiz2025benchmarking, p. 7; @deininger2026open, p. 8];
  - trivial baselines that are rarely reported but often competitive [@tashenova2026real, p. 8; @deininger2026open, p. 7; @lu2025lab, p. 14];
  - temporal and cross-dataset shift [@lu2025lab, p. 12; @lu2025lab, p. 13];
  - prompt wording and decision threshold, either of which can change the winner [@deininger2026open, p. 11; @deininger2026open, p. 20];
  - synthetic vs real code [@khare2025understanding, p. 5];
  - function vs statement granularity [@ahmed2026secvuleval, p. 8];
  - custom datasets with inconsistent splits [@kaniewski2026systematic, p. 35]. The same model on the same named dataset can differ by 28 F1 points between papers [@lu2025lab, p. 12; @ding2025vulnerability, p. 7].

  **Strength: strong** for label noise and the collapse under realistic evaluation, which are consistent across independent primary studies and two SLRs. **Moderate** for prompt and threshold sensitivity and for trivial baselines, which rest on fewer studies.

## RQ3: Do hybrid LLM + static-analysis approaches outperform either family alone, and what are the trade-offs in cost, speed and explainability?
- Type: comparative
- Why it matters: industry is moving to combined tools; evidence for their benefit is scattered.
- Linked objective: 3
- Status: answered (synthesis 2026-11-12; updated pass 2, 2026-11-12)
- Current answer (updated pass 2, 2026-11-12): LLM filtering of SAST alarms removes most false alarms at < $0.20 per alarm but loses 12–25% of real bugs [@du2026reducing, p. 8; @xiong2026sifting, p. 18] (**moderate**). Hybrid detection (IRIS, RepoAudit) beats SAST in the authors' own evaluations [@li2024iris, p. 8; @guo2025repoaudit, p. 8] but not in an independent project-scale study [@li2026llm, p. 7; @li2026llm, p. 12], and it needs a strong LLM [@li2024iris, p. 10] (**weak/contested**). LLM costs range from cents per alarm to $1,247.66 per project, against minutes and no token cost for SAST [@li2026llm, p. 16]. The only cost per true positive comes from a non-hybrid study: USD 0.003–0.212 and 3.7–119.6 kJ per true positive at 1:44 prevalence [@deininger2026open, p. 8]. These are compute costs only. Human triage of the 25.6–41.9 false positives per true one is excluded [@deininger2026open, p. 8], and part of the energy values are FLOP estimates [@deininger2026open, p. 14]. The same study finds that small open models can match a frontier model at 1/66 of the cost [@deininger2026open, p. 17] (**moderate, single study**). Explainability is limited: LLMs mislocate findings [@gnieciak2025large, p. 11], and fluent explanations do not signal correctness [@tan2026think, p. 9].

## RQ4: Which vulnerability types (CWEs), languages and code scales does each family handle well or poorly?
- Type: comparative
- Why it matters: guides tool choice for a given codebase and points to where research is missing.
- Linked objective: 1, 4
- Status: answered (synthesis 2026-11-12; updated pass 2, 2026-11-12)
- Current answer (updated pass 2, 2026-11-12): LLMs do best on self-contained injection-style CWEs and worst on context-dependent and crypto/policy CWEs and on localising memory-safety bugs [@khare2025understanding, p. 7; @xiong2026sifting, p. 18; @shu2026evaluating, p. 30]. CodeQL is better on CWE-190 [@khare2025understanding, p. 8]. LLM performance falls with function/file length [@ahmed2026secvuleval, p. 13; @li2026llm, p. 9]. Results also vary by project: LineVul (BigVul) finds 76% of Chromium CVEs but none in Linux, while all LLMs cover Linux better [@lu2025lab, p. 13]. Evidence is concentrated on C/C++ and Java [@kaniewski2026systematic, p. 28]. The only PHP evidence (tashenova2026real) tests no LLM and uses a bespoke lexical taint analyser that detects 1.7–1.8% of vulnerabilities. In that study, learned models detect XSS (F1 up to 0.500) far better than SQLi (≤ 0.146) or RCE (≤ 0.085) [@tashenova2026real, p. 9]. **Strength: weak** (small per-CWE cells, divergent results, a single PHP study).

## RQ5: What gaps and open problems remain for reliable, deployable LLM-based vulnerability detection?
- Type: descriptive
- Why it matters: basis for the gap section and future work of the paper.
- Linked objective: 4
- Status: answered (synthesis 2026-11-12; updated pass 2, 2026-11-12)
- Current answer (updated pass 2, 2026-11-12): Recurring open problems:
  - verified, realistic, repository-level benchmarks with context [@zhou2025large, p. 19; @sheng2025llms, p. 25];
  - trivial baselines and realistic prevalence in every evaluation [@tashenova2026real, p. 13; @deininger2026open, p. 8];
  - label quality and contamination control [@tashenova2026real, p. 12; @lu2025lab, p. 17];
  - same-benchmark LLM vs SAST vs DL comparisons. Two studies run all three: khare, with SAST on synthetic data only [@khare2025understanding, p. 7], and deininger, with lightweight function-level SAST on pre-2023 CVEs [@deininger2026open, p. 10; @deininger2026open, p. 24];
  - independent labelling and statistical testing, including prompt sensitivity [@li2026llm, p. 11; @deininger2026open, p. 20];
  - cost reporting that includes human triage and states where energy figures come from [@deininger2026open, p. 8];
  - true-positive loss in filtering [@xiong2026sifting, p. 19];
  - workflow integration [@zhou2025large, p. 19].

  deininger's authors judge that no tested method is ready for independent function-level triage [@deininger2026open, p. 26]. **Strength: moderate** [I]. These are candidate gaps only. Gap specifics and novelty checks are in gap-report.md.
