Vulnerability Detection: Re-Evaluating Static and
Learning-Based Detectors Under Leakage-Controlled Evaluation

                                         Abstract
                                         Background: WordPress plugins account for the large majority of disclosed CMS vul-
                                         nerabilities, and learning-based detectors report high accuracy on synthetic corpora and
                                         random splits. Methods: We build a benchmark from 1757 real plugin CVEs (6666 indexed
                                         identifiers, 1281 plugins), yielding 30,860 labeled PHP functions in two negative-sampling
                                         variants. An audit of the released artifacts found exact normalized-code duplicates crossing
                                         test folds (166 groups/1086 records in Variant A; 151/676 in Variant B) and identical code
                                         carrying contradictory labels (730 and 456 groups). After removing conflicting groups,
                                         merging exact duplicates and collapsing token- and AST-level clone families, the corpora
                                         contain 15,294 (Variant A) and 24,917 (Variant B) records with zero shared hashes, clone
                                         families or plugins across folds. Ten detectors are compared under plugin-grouped five-
                                         fold cross-validation on complete held-out folds: a static taint analyzer, TF-IDF Random
                                         Forest and SVM, Bi-LSTM, unidirectional LSTM, a taint-augmented neural model, a nested
                                         out-of-fold stacking ensemble, and three baselines (majority class, a source/sink presence
                                         rule, and a single-feature function-length classifier). Results: The best macro-F1 is 0.563
                                         (Variant A, binary, SVM) and 0.601 (Variant B, binary, nested stacking). Neural models do
                                         not lead in any of the six configurations, and the single-feature length baseline matches
                                         or exceeds the TF-IDF Random Forest in four of six. The source/sink rule attains an MCC
                                         close to zero, and the taint analyzer misses about 98% of vulnerabilities. Manual review
                                         of 400 functions finds 48.8% of cleaned positive labels to be genuine vulnerabilities. Con-
                                         clusions: Under duplicate-controlled evaluation, the differences between detector families
                                         are small compared with the variation attributable to label quality, which appears to be
                                         the more binding constraint. All data, folds, predictions and fingerprinted result files

                                       third-party plugins lag far behind and account for the overwhelming majority of reported
                                       vulnerabilities [1,2]. Cross-site scripting (XSS) and SQL injection (SQLi) dominate the
                                       reported classes, with a smaller but more severe tail of remote code execution (RCE) [3].
                                            Prevailing defenses are predominantly reactive. Signature-based scanners and web
                                       application firewalls act after a vulnerability is public, and vulnerability databases catalog
                                       flaws only after disclosure. This motivates proactive detection directly on plugin source
                                       code. In earlier work we proposed SentinelCMS [4], a detector combining static taint
                                       analysis with a bidirectional LSTM, which reached approximately 93% accuracy on a
                                       synthetic corpus of 600 generated snippets.
                                            Whether such results transfer to real, disclosed vulnerabilities is the question this
                                       paper addresses. We assemble a benchmark from real plugin CVEs, evaluate a range of
                                       detector families on it, and—prompted by an audit of our own released artifacts—pay
                                       particular attention to duplication and evaluation leakage, which turned out to affect the
                                       measurements substantially.
                                            An audit of the first release showed that grouping cross-validation folds by plugin slug
                                       does not prevent exact code duplicates from crossing fold boundaries, because WordPress
                                       plugins routinely vendor shared libraries and administrative menus. It also showed that
                                       identical functions sometimes carry contradictory class labels, and that a stacking ensemble
                                       described as out-of-fold was in fact trained on in-sample predictions. The present version
                                       rebuilds the corpus and the entire benchmark with these problems corrected, and quantifies
                                       how much they inflated the earlier numbers.
                                            We address four research questions. RQ1 (transfer): does training on real CVE data
                                       outperform training on synthetic data? RQ2 (detector families): how do static, classical
                                       and neural detectors compare under duplicate-controlled evaluation? RQ3 (negatives and
                                       granularity): how do the negative-sampling variant and label granularity affect the results?
                                       RQ4 (evaluation validity): how much do duplicate leakage and in-sample stacking inflate
                                       reported performance?
                                            This study makes four contributions. (1) A real, duplicate-controlled benchmark of
                                       WordPress plugin CVEs with verified zero overlap of code hashes, clone families and
                                       plugins across folds. (2) A comparison of ten detectors, including three deliberately trivial
                                       baselines, on complete held-out folds. (3) A direct measurement of how much duplicate
                                       leakage and in-sample stacking inflate performance. (4) An expert validation of the la-
                                       bel pipeline on 400 manually reviewed functions. All artifacts are released with input
                                       fingerprints so that any result can be traced to the data from which it was computed.

                                       2. Related Work
                                       2.1. Vulnerabilities in CMS Ecosystems
                                            Large-scale measurements of deployed websites show that outdated third-party com-
                                       ponents, rather than CMS cores, dominate the exposure surface [1,5]. Industry reports on
                                       the WordPress ecosystem quantify the same pattern, with plugins responsible for the large
                                       majority of newly disclosed issues each year [2].

                                       2.2. Static Analysis for PHP
                                            Static detection of injection flaws in PHP has a long history, from early taint-tracking
                                       tools such as Pixy [6] to graph-based frameworks. Code-property-graph approaches [7]
                                       generalize this by combining syntax, control flow and data dependence in a single queryable
                                       structure. Such analyzers are precise when the vulnerable data flow is local and syntactically
                                       explicit, but degrade on dynamic dispatch, framework callbacks and flows that cross file
                                       boundaries—all common in plugin code.

                                       2.3. Learning-Based Vulnerability Detection
                                            VulDeePecker [8] introduced slice-level neural detection; graph neural networks (e.g.,
                                       Devign [9]) encode structural dependencies; and line-level transformer variants such as
                                       LineVul [10] report F1 above 0.90 on Big-Vul under random splits. Most of this work targets
                                       C/C++. For PHP, DeepTective [11] combines gated graph networks with sequence models
                                       on a corpus assembled from plugin sources.

                                       2.4. Pre-Trained Code Models
                                            CodeBERT [12] provides bimodal pre-training over code and natural language,
                                       GraphCodeBERT [13] injects data-flow structure, and instruction-tuned large language
                                       models [14] have been applied to vulnerability reasoning. On established benchmarks such
                                       as CodeXGLUE [15] these models reach roughly 62% accuracy on defect detection, a level
                                       far below the numbers reported on curated corpora.

                                       2.5. Data Quality and Evaluation Validity
                                            A growing body of work questions whether reported gains reflect detection ability or
                                       evaluation artifacts. Chakraborty et al. [16] show that models trained on curated datasets
                                       degrade sharply on real-world code. Arp et al. [17] catalog recurring methodological
                                       pitfalls in machine learning for security, including sampling bias and spurious correla-
                                       tions. Croft et al. [18] measure label accuracy and duplication in widely used vulnerability
                                       datasets and find both to be substantial problems, and Ding et al. [19] report that bench-
                                       mark scores fall considerably once duplicates and unrealistic splits are removed. The
                                       present study contributes a domain-specific instance of this critique, with a measured
                                       decomposition of duplication, label noise and ensemble leakage in a PHP/CMS corpus.

                                       3. Materials and Methods
                                       3.1. Dataset Construction
                                            Candidate vulnerabilities were indexed from the NVD CVE API v2 [20] and the
                                       public CVE list (CVEProject/cvelistV5) [21]. The two sources overlap because the same
                                       CVE is frequently listed in both; after removing duplicate identifiers the merged index
                                       contains 6666 unique records, which reconciles the 5839 and 853 source counts (26 shared
                                       identifiers). Of these, 1757 CVEs across 1281 plugins could be resolved to downloadable
                                       pre-fix and post-fix source pairs (1234 of these plugins still carry vulnerable records after
                                       the deduplication of Section 3.2); approximately 54% of indexed CVEs were unrecoverable
                                       because old releases and SVN tags had been pruned.
                                            Function-level labels were derived by differencing the vulnerable and patched versions
                                       with a tree-sitter PHP parser: functions touched by the security fix receive the CVE’s
                                       vulnerability type, and untouched functions provide negatives. Two negative-sampling
                                       variants are retained: Variant A uses the patched twin of each vulnerable function, and
                                       Variant B uses functions from the same plugin that were not modified by the fix. Each
                                       variant contains 30,860 snippets before deduplication. A per-record provenance manifest
                                       (CVE id, source, plugin slug, version, file, function name and code hash) accompanies
                                       the release.
                                            The dataset construction and leakage-control procedure used in this study is illustrated
                                       in Figure 1. The workflow shows how function-level labels are derived from vulnerable
                                       and patched releases, followed by consistency checks, duplicate removal, clone-family
                                       auditing, and fold assignment to minimize potential data leakage.

                                       3.2. Duplicate Audit and Leakage Control
                                            Cross-validation folds in the first release partitioned plugin slugs. An audit of the
                                       published code_hash fields showed that this is insufficient: identical normalized code
                                       appearing under two different plugin slugs is split across training and test folds. Table 1
                                       reports the audit. Beyond exact duplicates, identical code was found carrying contradictory
                                       class labels, because diff-based labeling assigns the CVE’s type to every changed function,
                                       so a function touched by several CVEs receives several types.

                                             Three corrections were applied before any model was retrained. First, every hash
                                       group carrying contradictory labels was removed in full: identical code with mutually
                                       exclusive labels is unreliable supervision, and retaining either side would inject known-
                                       incorrect labels. Second, exact duplicates were merged into a single record. Third, near-
                                       duplicates were identified at both the token level (MinHash over 5-token shingles, Jaccard
                                       similiarity of at least 0.9) and the AST level (tree-sitter node-type sequences, Jaccard
                                       similiarity of at least 0.9) and grouped into clone families.
                                             Grouping folds by the connected components of plugin slug and clone family is
                                       leakage-free by construction but produces a giant component: cross-plugin copy-paste,

                                       fold statistics become meaningless. We therefore collapse each clone family to a single
                                       representative record, after which a family exists only once and grouping by plugin slug
                                       suffices. Table 2 reports the resulting corpora. The merged-component grouping was
                                       therefore rejected rather than adopted, and the decision, together with the component
                                       statistics, is recorded in the released collapse report; the collapsed corpora are the ones

                                       3.3. Detectors and Baselines
                                            Ten configurations are evaluated. Static taint analyzer: a two-pass intra-procedural
                                       analyzer that propagates taint from user-controlled sources and reports a finding when
                                       a tainted value reaches a security-sensitive sink without sanitization. TF-IDF classifiers:
                                       Random Forest and a linear-kernel SVM over character n-gram TF-IDF features (char_wb
                                       analyzer, 2–4 g over the raw code). Neural models: a Bi-LSTM with attention, a unidirec-
                                       tional ablation, and a combined model that concatenates the taint verdict to the Bi-LSTM
                                       representation. Stacking: a logistic-regression meta-learner over base-model predictions,
                                       evaluated in two forms described below.
                                            Three baselines are added deliberately because a benchmark is only informative if
                                       trivial predictors are shown to be insufficient. The majority baseline always predicts the
                                       most frequent training class. The source/sink baseline predicts a vulnerability whenever
                                       a taint source and a type-matching sink are both present—this is exactly the rule used
                                       to clean labels in Section 4.5, so its standalone detection ability is directly relevant. The
                                       function-length baseline is a logistic regression on a single feature, the number of tokens in
                                       the function.
                                            The stacking ensemble is reported in two forms. The in-sample form reproduces the
                                       originally released implementation, in which the base models are fitted on the complete
                                       outer training fold and their predictions on those same records become the meta-features.
                                       The nested form generates meta-features strictly out of fold, using an inner Stratified-
                                       GroupKFold within each outer training fold, and refits the base models on the full outer
                                       training fold only to score the test fold. Only the nested form is citable; the in-sample
                                       form is retained solely to quantify the bias in Section 4.4. Both forms use the same base

                                       3.4. Experimental Protocol
                                            All models are evaluated with StratifiedGroupKFold (it is the cross-validation splitter
                                       from scikit-learn version 1.8.0.) (five folds, seed 42) grouped by plugin slug on the collapsed
                                       corpora. Test folds are always the complete held-out set: no balancing or subsampling
                                       is applied to any test fold, so all reported metrics are prevalence-preserving. Where the
                                       training budget required it, neural models subsample the training side only (at most
                                       4000 records per fold, 40 epochs, sequence length 128); each such decision is recorded
                                       in the released sampling manifest. The 128-token bound was chosen for computational
                                       tractability on a CPU-only host rather than by tuning, so that the sequence models could
                                       be trained across all folds and configurations within the available budget; its effect on
                                       the ranking has not been measured, and a 256-token sensitivity analysis is reported as
                                       outstanding in Section 5.2. Configurations span the two variants and three granularities:
                                       binary (Safe versus vulnerable), three-class (Safe, SQLi, XSS) and four-class (adding RCE).
                                            We report accuracy and macro-F1 as fold means with standard deviation and 95%
                                       confidence intervals (t distribution, four degrees of freedom), per-class precision, recall and
                                       F1, vulnerable-class recall and false-negative rate, balanced accuracy, Matthews correlation
                                       coefficient and PR-AUC. Model comparisons use paired tests both across folds and across
                                       plugins, with Cohen’s d as effect size; the plugin-level test is the better-powered of the
                                       two, since fold-level tests have only five paired observations. Per-fold predictions and class
                                       scores are released, so every reported number can be recomputed.
                                            Result files are keyed to input fingerprints. Each artifact records the SHA-256 of the
                                       dataset index and of the fold assignment file, and a cached result is reused only if both
                                       match the current inputs. This replaces the earlier checkpoint logic, which was keyed on a
                                       filename tag and silently reused results computed on an older and smaller corpus.

                                       4.3. Per-Class Behavior and Degenerate Predictors

                                       5. Discussion
                                       5.1. Comparison with Reported Results
                                            The gap between our figures and the higher numbers reported elsewhere is best
                                       explained by methodology rather than model quality. Random splits allow near-duplicate
                                       functions to appear in both training and test, which our audit shows is not a hypothetical
                                       concern in this domain: even after grouping by plugin, 1086 records in Variant A were
                                       exact copies of training material. Our labels are derived from security-fix diffs and, as the
                                       expert validation shows, are correct for only about half of the positive class. Both factors
                                       push measured performance down relative to curated corpora, and both are properties of
                                       the data rather than of the detectors.
                                            Taken together, the results suggest a reordering of priorities. The spread between
                                       the best and worst learned models within a setting is typically smaller than the spread
                                       introduced by changing the negative-sampling variant, and a single-feature baseline sits
                                       within that spread. Architecture and representation choices therefore appear to be second-
                                       order in this domain, while label quality—measured at 48.8% precision on the cleaned
                                       positive class—is the more plausible binding constraint. We state this as the most consistent

                                       reading of the evidence rather than as a demonstrated causal claim, since the label-cleaning
                                       comparison that would establish it directly is confounded by subset difficulty (Section 4.5).

                                       5.2. Threats to Validity
                                            The protocol is duplicate-controlled rather than provably leakage-free. Exact dupli-
                                       cates and token- and AST-level clone families are removed, and the released folds share no
                                       hash, clone family or plugin, but semantic clones that differ lexically and structurally may
                                       persist. Collapsing clone families also changes the class balance, most sharply in Variant
                                       A, where the majority class becomes the vulnerable class; accuracy is therefore not compa-
                                       rable with the earlier release and macro-F1, balanced accuracy and MCC should be used
                                       for comparison.
                                            The dataset is incomplete: about 54% of indexed CVEs were unrecoverable because
                                       pre-fix releases and old SVN tags were pruned, so the corpus may be biased toward plugins
                                       that retain historical versions. Rare-class estimates are unreliable—RCE has 848 records
                                       in Variant B and several models never predict it—so four-class results should be treated
                                       as indicative. The neural models were trained on at most 4000 records per fold for 40
                                       epochs under a CPU budget, so their numbers are a lower bound; the test folds, however,
                                       are always complete. A transformer comparison is absent: the transformers library is not
                                       installed in the evaluation environment and the host has no GPU, so CodeBERT was not
                                       run and is not reported. A 256-token sensitivity run for the sequence models is likewise
                                       outstanding. Finally, the taint analyzer is intra-procedural and lexical, so its low recall
                                       reflects that implementation rather than taint analysis in general.

                                       6. Limitations and Future Work
                                             The central limitation is that the labels themselves are only about half correct on the
                                       positive class, which bounds what any model trained on them can demonstrate. The priority
                                       is therefore data rather than architecture. First, expert-verified data at scale: extending the
                                       400-function review to several thousand functions with at least two annotators, adjudication
                                       and reported agreement, and evaluating on a genuinely expert-verified test set. Second,
                                       curated vulnerability feeds the supply entry point and sink explicitly, reducing reliance on
                                       diff heuristics. Third, semantic clone detection beyond token and AST similarity, since the
                                       present analysis removes only lexical and structural duplicates. Fourth, the outstanding
                                       runs: a fully resourced transformer comparison on identical folds and the 256-token
                                       sensitivity analysis. Fifth, given that a single-feature length baseline is competitive, future
                                       detectors in this domain should be required to clear such trivial baselines before semantic
                                       detection is claimed.

                                       7. Conclusions
                                             We presented a duplicate-controlled benchmark of real WordPress plugin vulnerabil-
                                       ities and re-evaluated ten detectors on it. An audit of our own first release showed that
                                       plugin-level grouping does not prevent exact code duplicates from crossing fold boundaries,
                                       that identical code sometimes carries contradictory labels, and that a stacking ensemble
                                       described as out-of-fold was trained in sample. After correcting all three, the corpora
                                       contain 15,294 and 24,917 records with verified zero overlap across folds.
                                             On these corpora the best macro-F1 is 0.563 and 0.601 in the binary settings. No neural
                                       configuration leads in any of the six settings, a single-feature function-length classifier
                                       matches or exceeds the TF-IDF Random Forest in four of six, the source/sink rule has
                                       essentially no standalone detection ability, and the static analyzer misses about 98% of
                                       vulnerabilities. In-sample stacking is optimistic by 1.27 percentage points on average.
                                       Expert review finds 48.8% of the cleaned positive labels to be genuine.
